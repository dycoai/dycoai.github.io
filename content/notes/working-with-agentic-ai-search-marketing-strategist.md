# 1. Working with Agentic AI: Search Marketing Strategist

_How human skills and agentic skills are dynamically integrated and coordinated across search marketing responsibilities_

## 1.1. Search Marketing Strategist as an Occupation

A **search marketing strategist** develops, implements, and optimizes search engine marketing (SEM) and search engine optimization (SEO) strategies to increase online visibility, drive targeted traffic, and maximize return on investment. This occupation connects organizational objectives with how people search by defining campaign structures, optimizing digital assets, and analyzing search behavior and performance across paid and organic channels. To ensure search strategies support long-term organizational objectives, the strategist must understand how search engine systems, user intent, competitive conditions, technical site infrastructure, and budget constraints collectively affect search visibility, campaign performance, and business outcomes.

Typical responsibilities of a search marketing strategist include:

- search strategy and campaign architecture;
- keyword research and semantic mapping;
- technical SEO and site audit management;
- content optimization and search intent alignment;
- paid search (PPC) bidding and budget allocation;
- competitor analysis and market positioning;
- performance analytics and attribution modeling; and
- algorithm adaptation and search trend forecasting.

These responsibilities are fulfilled through distinct tasks and subtasks, each requiring one or more actions with specific skill requirements. Human skills and agentic skills both contribute to these responsibilities, but their levels and forms of involvement differ according to the responsibility, task, action, campaign scope, market context, algorithm complexity, technical environment, and expected outcome.

## 1.2. Human Skills and Agentic Skills in Search Marketing

Search marketing increasingly integrates both **human skills** and **agentic skills**.

Human skills are reusable capabilities possessed and developed by search marketing strategists. They include strategic reasoning, search intent interpretation, audience understanding, quantitative reasoning, analytical interpretation, experimentation, technical reasoning, brand judgment, stakeholder communication, contextual reasoning, and professional judgment.

Agentic skills are reusable capabilities available to AI agents through their underlying models, contextual information, instructions, constraints, tools, and computational resources. In search marketing, these skills include data extraction, keyword clustering, bid calculation, site crawling, anomaly detection, trend summarization, report generation, A/B test simulation, code snippet generation, and tool use.

Both skill types contribute to the same occupational responsibilities, but their involvement varies by action. Defining a search campaign's strategic direction relies primarily on human understanding of brand positioning and consumer behavior; an AI agent then uses agentic skills to retrieve historical search volume data and organize competitive benchmarks. In technical SEO, the strategist first defines the crawl parameters and business priorities, and an AI agent subsequently crawls the site infrastructure, identifies indexing errors, and maps redirect chains. For performance reporting, an AI agent aggregates cross-channel attribution data, while the strategist interprets the business impact, evaluates assisted conversions, and determines the appropriate budget reallocation. Across these actions, human and agentic skills are activated sequentially or iteratively based on task requirements, with the human professional maintaining oversight and final judgment.

The relationship between these skills is not a fixed division where certain responsibilities permanently belong to humans and others to AI. Instead, human and agentic involvement shifts across tasks and between different actions within the same task.

## 1.3. Dynamic Human–Agentic Skill Integration and Collaboration in Search Marketing

The work of a search marketing strategist can be understood as a structured set of responsibilities fulfilled through tasks and subtasks, which are executed through actions requiring appropriate configurations of human and agentic skills.

An **occupation** provides the overall professional context. For a search marketing strategist, this context encompasses aligning business goals with search engine visibility, managing paid and organic search portfolios, optimizing digital content for user intent, and maximizing the efficiency of digital marketing investments.

A **responsibility** represents an expected area of professional work or an outcome that the search marketing strategist accomplishes or maintains. Responsibilities such as campaign architecture, technical SEO, bid management, and performance analytics each contain multiple tasks.

A **task** is a **context-dependent unit of work** used to fulfill part or all of a responsibility. When a task is too broad or complex for reliable execution, it is decomposed into smaller **subtasks**.

Tasks and subtasks are executed through one or more **actions**. Actions represent **reusable and configurable operations** such as clustering keyword themes, calculating bid multipliers, auditing site architecture, analyzing competitor ad copy, identifying traffic anomalies, or generating content optimization briefs.

Each action has **skill requirements**. These requirements describe the capabilities needed for effective and reliable execution. Depending on the action and its context, human skills, agentic skills, or an integration of both can satisfy these requirements.

The skills available for search marketing reside in a **skill space** containing both human skills and agentic skills. Relevant skills in this space can serve as candidate skills for executing the actions required by a particular task. A human skill in consumer psychology, for example, can serve as a candidate for interpreting the underlying intent behind a complex search query, while an agentic skill in keyword clustering can serve as a candidate for grouping thousands of related queries into thematic ad groups.

Candidate skills are evaluated relative to the requirements of the action and its occupational and task context. Appropriate skills are then selected and organized into a **skill configuration** for execution. Depending on these requirements, a configuration may rely mainly on human skills, mainly on agentic skills, or on an integrated arrangement of both.

### 1.3.1. Skills Competition and Skills Collaboration

Two important relationships can occur between human skills and agentic skills when constructing a skill configuration: **skills competition** and **skills collaboration**.

**Skills competition** occurs when both a human skill and an agentic skill can satisfy the same or similar requirements for an action, but simultaneous application of both is unnecessary. Under a particular occupational and task context, one skill type can provide a better fit for the action than the other.

For example, both a search marketing strategist and an AI agent can generate standard meta descriptions for a large catalog of product pages. When the page content is explicit, the character limits are strictly defined, and the primary keywords are known, the agentic skill provides fast and consistent execution at scale. When the meta description requires capturing a nuanced brand voice, addressing a sensitive consumer pain point, or differentiating a highly commoditized product, the strategist's human skills provide the creative and contextual reasoning required for the action.

The purpose of skills competition is not to establish whether human or agentic skills are generally superior. Rather, it identifies which available skill provides the better fit for a particular action under particular conditions.

**Skills collaboration** occurs when one skill type alone cannot effectively satisfy the requirements of a task or its actions, or when combining complementary human and agentic skills produces stronger task performance.

Collaboration does not require human and agentic skills to operate simultaneously. Instead, they are activated at different stages of action execution in a **sequential, iterative, or recursive manner** according to evolving task requirements.

For example, a search marketing strategist first identifies a sudden drop in organic traffic for a core service category and determines which algorithmic or competitive variables require investigation. An AI agent then analyzes search performance data, compares competitor ranking changes and search engine results page (SERP) features, and identifies patterns associated with a recent change in search results. The strategist subsequently interprets these findings in relation to evolving user search intent and develops several content pivot strategies. Based on the strategist's direction, the agent next generates targeted content briefs and semantic keyword maps to address the new intent. Finally, the strategist evaluates the resulting content strategy and determines which assets should be prioritized for immediate optimization. Throughout this sequence, human and agentic skills are activated iteratively as task requirements evolve, while the strategist directs the analysis and evaluates its outcomes.

This dynamic relationship allows search marketing strategists to leverage the complementary strengths of both skill types rather than relying on a permanent allocation of work.

### 1.3.2. Skill Configuration and Task Execution

A **skill configuration** specifies which human skills and agentic skills are used to execute the actions associated with a task or subtask and how those skills are coordinated.

The configuration depends on factors such as action requirements, campaign budget, algorithmic volatility, competitive density, seasonal trends, expected reliability, brand safety constraints, organizational impact, decision consequences, stakeholder expectations, available tools, and intermediate results.

For one action, an agentic skill may independently satisfy the requirements. For another, a human skill may provide the better fit. A complex search marketing task may require several configurations across multiple actions, with human and agentic skills repeatedly interacting as the task progresses.

The configuration is therefore dynamic rather than permanent. It may change when the action changes, new search trend data becomes available, market conditions shift, an agentic result fails to satisfy expected requirements, or human interpretation identifies a new strategic direction.

Throughout this process, the search marketing strategist remains the professional responsible for directing and controlling the work associated with the occupation and its responsibilities. Agentic AI provides powerful complementary capabilities that expand what the professional can crawl, cluster, calculate, analyze, simulate, audit, and communicate.

### 1.3.3. Task Performance Evaluation and Skill Space Adaptation

The performance produced through a selected skill configuration is evaluated against **predefined evaluation metrics**. These metrics determine whether the human skills, agentic skills, or their integrated configuration are sufficiently capable of performing the relevant task and its required actions.

Evaluation criteria depend on the work being performed. In search marketing, they include organic visibility, qualified traffic, click-through rate (CTR), conversion rate, cost per acquisition (CPA), return on ad spend (ROAS), technical issue resolution, revenue contribution, and other task-specific expectations.

When a selected skill or skill configuration produces performance above the defined threshold, it passes the performance evaluation and is retained for future applications under appropriate conditions. Repeated successful use provides additional evidence about where that skill or configuration performs effectively.

When performance falls below the required threshold, the corresponding skill or configuration is not reused without modification. The relevant human or agentic skills may be refined or reconfigured, existing skills can be combined differently, or new human and agentic skills may be developed or constructed from scratch when the existing skill space lacks adequate capability.

For example, an AI agent first generates automated bid adjustments for a paid search portfolio, but task performance evaluation shows that it repeatedly over-invests in informational queries with low conversion propensity during seasonal peak periods. The agentic skill is then refined through improved contextual instructions, integration with seasonal business calendars, stronger intent-classification procedures, or validation against historical conversion margins. The revised skill is evaluated again, and once it satisfies the required performance criteria, it is retained for appropriate future bid-management tasks. Human skills follow a corresponding development process: when a strategist lacks sufficient knowledge of a newly released search engine advertising format, the relevant capability is developed through learning, experimentation, and practical experience before its performance is evaluated through subsequent work.

Refined and newly constructed skills that satisfy the required performance criteria are added to the skill space. In this way, task performance evaluation provides feedback not only for completing current work but also for continuously optimizing and expanding the available skill space.

The skill space is not constructed as an isolated collection of occupation-specific representations. Many human and agentic skills used in search marketing are reusable across occupations. Strategic reasoning, data extraction, anomaly detection, trend summarization, A/B test simulation, report generation, and tool use contribute to work in social media management, e-commerce merchandising, product management, user experience research, management consulting, and many other fields.

Reusable skills can therefore be represented once and configured differently according to the responsibilities, tasks, actions, and contexts of different occupations. This allows the skill space to serve a growing range of occupations without unnecessarily duplicating or exploding the number of skill representations. Occupational specialization is introduced through task context, action requirements, campaign parameters, information sources, tools, constraints, and skill configurations rather than by creating a completely separate skill representation whenever the same underlying capability appears in another occupation.

The resulting process is adaptive:

**occupational responsibility → task and subtask → actions → skill requirements → candidate human and agentic skills → skill configuration → task execution → task performance evaluation → skill refinement, reconfiguration, or construction → optimized and expanded skill space**

This cycle allows human and agentic capabilities to evolve alongside the requirements of search marketing work.


## 1.4. Essential Responsibilities of Search Marketing Strategist

### 1.4.1. Search Strategy and Campaign Architecture

Search strategy and campaign architecture establishes the foundational structure and strategic direction required to capture digital demand across paid and organic channels. This responsibility includes understanding business objectives, assessing current digital visibility, defining target audiences, establishing paid campaign structures and organic search priorities, and determining how search portfolios should evolve over time.

Agentic skills contribute to historical data extraction, market volume summarization, account structure generation, scenario simulation, and comparison of architectural alternatives. Human skills remain essential for interpreting brand positioning, balancing short-term acquisition with long-term brand building, determining acceptable budget trade-offs, and defining a search strategy that remains realistic within market constraints.

For example, a search marketing strategist first defines the revenue objectives and target audience segments that the future search portfolio must support. An AI agent then examines available historical search data, competitor activity estimates, and seasonal trend reports, summarizing significant market opportunities and volume constraints. The strategist interprets these findings and develops several candidate search portfolio directions. Based on the strategist's criteria, the agent next compares these alternatives across projected reach, cost efficiency, and implementation complexity. Finally, the strategist resolves the major trade-offs and establishes the search architecture that guides subsequent execution.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|strategic reasoning, brand alignment, audience psychology, trade-off evaluation, portfolio design|data extraction, trend summarization, structure generation, scenario simulation, comparative analysis|The strategist defines strategic search direction and evaluates major trade-offs, while the AI agent organizes market evidence, identifies opportunities, and compares architectural alternatives.|

### 1.4.2. Keyword Research and Semantic Mapping

Keyword research and semantic mapping translates search behavior into structured thematic groups that align with user intent, customer journeys, and organizational offerings. This responsibility includes identifying seed queries, expanding keyword lists, clustering semantic themes, mapping keywords to specific landing pages, and identifying gaps in current market coverage.

Agentic skills are well suited to extracting search queries from analytics platforms, clustering thousands of keywords by semantic similarity, mapping search volumes, comparing historical rankings, and linking queries to relevant site assets. Human skills contribute to determining the intent behind ambiguous queries, resolving overlapping semantic themes, defining future-state content targets, and deciding how keyword priorities should influence budget allocation.

For example, an AI agent first extracts relevant search queries from analytics and search console platforms, preparing a preliminary semantic clustering based on natural language processing. The search marketing strategist reviews the clusters and identifies areas where the agent groups distinct search intents into overly broad categories. The strategist then restructures the map around distinct search intents and relevant stages of the customer journey. Based on the revised structure, the agent maps supporting landing pages, calculates aggregate search volumes, and identifies coverage gaps. Finally, the strategist validates the resulting semantic map and determines which keyword themes require immediate campaign development.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|intent interpretation, semantic reasoning, strategic prioritization, buyer journey mapping, gap analysis|query extraction, semantic clustering, volume calculation, cross-referencing, mapping documentation|The AI agent accelerates extraction and clustering of search data, while the strategist defines the intent structure and determines its strategic significance.|

### 1.4.3. Technical SEO and Site Audit Management

Technical SEO and site audit management evaluates and improves the technical conditions that affect how search engines crawl, index, and interpret organizational web assets. This responsibility includes tracking site health, identifying crawl errors, examining Core Web Vitals, evaluating site architecture, and establishing technical remediation priorities.

Agentic skills efficiently crawl large website architectures, extract rendering errors, identify broken links, map redirect chains, summarize page speed metrics, and identify candidates for technical optimization. Human skills remain essential for understanding the business criticality of specific page templates, evaluating the development cost of technical fixes, determining whether apparent errors represent intentional design choices, and establishing engineering priorities.

For example, the search marketing strategist first defines the criteria for reviewing the technical health of a major web property, including crawl depth limits, rendering requirements, and Core Web Vitals thresholds. An AI agent then crawls the site against these criteria and identifies potential areas of indexation bloat and rendering failures. The strategist reviews the identified candidates and distinguishes genuine technical debt from legacy templates that are scheduled for imminent retirement. The agent next analyzes the internal link equity flow for the selected technical issues. Finally, the strategist establishes recommendations for engineering remediation, prioritization, or architectural restructuring.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|technical prioritization, engineering coordination, site architecture evaluation, business criticality assessment, remediation strategy|site crawling, error extraction, redirect mapping, speed analysis, indexation auditing|The AI agent systematically crawls large web architectures and identifies potential technical errors, while the strategist determines which technical changes are appropriate within the wider engineering and business context.|

### 1.4.4. Content Optimization and Search Intent Alignment

Content optimization and search intent alignment ensures that digital assets satisfy the specific informational, navigational, or transactional needs of the searching user while adhering to search engine quality guidelines. This responsibility includes analyzing SERP features, assessing content depth, identifying topic and semantic gaps, evaluating readability, and guiding content teams toward appropriate optimization strategies.

Agentic skills support SERP feature extraction, semantic gap analysis, readability scoring, entity recognition, and content brief generation. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the nuanced psychological intent behind a search query, evaluating the required brand tone, resolving conflicts between SEO best practices and user experience, and controlling the final editorial direction.

For example, a content team first submits a draft article for a high-priority search query. The search marketing strategist establishes the target user intent, relevant topics and concepts, and expected depth of coverage. An AI agent then analyzes the draft against current high-ranking search results and identifies potentially important topic gaps, structural gaps, and readability issues. The strategist reviews these findings and determines which structural changes enhance the user narrative and which compromise the brand voice. Based on the additional direction, the agent performs another semantic comparison and generates a revised optimization checklist. Finally, the strategist evaluates the complete evidence and determines whether the content is ready for publication or requires further editorial revision.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|intent interpretation, editorial judgment, brand voice protection, user experience reasoning, narrative design|SERP extraction, semantic gap analysis, entity recognition, readability scoring, brief generation|The AI agent performs systematic SERP comparison and prepares optimization evidence, while the strategist interprets user intent, protects the brand voice, and controls the final editorial direction.|

### 1.4.5. Paid Search (PPC) Bidding and Budget Allocation

Paid search (PPC) bidding and budget allocation ensures that paid media investments are distributed efficiently across campaigns, ad groups, and search opportunities to maximize return on ad spend (ROAS) and achieve target acquisition costs. This responsibility includes defining bidding strategies, evaluating auction dynamics, identifying budget bottlenecks, configuring bidding objectives and constraints, and ensuring that paid search decisions remain aligned with financial objectives.

Agentic skills assist with performance analysis, budget pacing analysis, anomaly detection, bid and target adjustment recommendations, and scenario evaluation. Human skills remain essential for determining strategic profit margins, evaluating the long-term value of specific customer segments, understanding the impact of external market shocks, and selecting bidding directions that remain consistent with overall business strategy.

For example, the search marketing strategist first defines the strategic ROAS targets, conversion values, budget limits, and brand safety constraints for an upcoming seasonal campaign. An AI agent then analyzes historical conversion and search-term performance and generates baseline recommendations for budget, bidding targets, and campaign-level adjustments. The strategist reviews the recommendations and identifies that the proposed allocation over-invests in informational queries with low conversion propensity that do not align with seasonal margin goals. Based on the strategist's revised parameters, the agent recalculates the targets and reallocates budget toward higher-intent search opportunities. Finally, the strategist validates the final budget distribution and authorizes the campaign launch.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|margin strategy, financial judgment, auction reasoning, segment valuation, risk-return trade-off analysis|performance analysis, bid and target adjustment, pacing analysis, anomaly detection, scenario generation|The strategist defines the financial requirements and evaluates strategic trade-offs, while the AI agent analyzes campaign performance and prepares bidding and budget adjustments needed for execution.|

### 1.4.6. Competitor Analysis and Market Positioning

Competitor analysis and market positioning establishes how the organization's search presence compares against key market rivals, identifying strategic gaps and opportunities for differentiation. This responsibility includes defining competitor sets, tracking share of voice, analyzing competitor ad copy and landing pages, identifying keyword overlap, and ensuring that search strategies support competitive differentiation.

Agentic skills efficiently retrieve competitor ad copy, track ranking fluctuations, detect new landing page launches, compare keyword overlap, and generate share-of-voice dashboards. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the strategic intent behind a competitor's search pivot, determining appropriate competitive responses, resolving cross-channel positioning conflicts, and ensuring that search strategies capitalize on competitor weaknesses.

For example, an AI agent first analyzes available competitor search data and identifies evidence of a substantial increase in a rival's paid search activity and a shift in its organic landing page messaging. The search marketing strategist reviews the resulting landscape and determines whether this represents a temporary promotional tactic or a more fundamental shift in the competitor's market positioning. The strategist then defines the target counter-strategy and unique value propositions required to defend market share. Based on this direction, the agent reorganizes the competitive tracking dashboard and generates new ad copy variations highlighting the organization's distinct advantages. Finally, the strategist validates the competitive response strategy and establishes the guidance required for the creative team.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|competitive reasoning, strategic positioning, market interpretation, counter-strategy design, differentiation judgment|ad copy retrieval, ranking tracking, overlap detection, dashboard generation, share-of-voice analysis|The AI agent systematically tracks and compares competitor search activities, while the strategist interprets the strategic intent and designs the competitive response required for market defense.|

### 1.4.7. Performance Analytics and Attribution Modeling

Performance analytics and attribution modeling ensures that search marketing investments are measured, interpreted, and connected as reliably as possible to downstream business outcomes. This responsibility includes evaluating attribution models, identifying tracking discrepancies, analyzing cross-channel assisted conversions, calculating customer lifetime value (CLV), and ensuring that analytics configurations support useful and defensible business reporting.

Agentic skills support data aggregation, attribution model simulation, discrepancy identification, cohort analysis, and dashboard generation. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the business validity of attribution models, resolving conflicts between marketing and finance reporting, understanding the offline impact of digital search, and determining the appropriate strategic response to performance data.

For example, the search marketing strategist first establishes the business requirements for measuring the contribution of upper-funnel organic search visibility to downstream conversions. An AI agent then aggregates cross-channel analytics data, compares alternative attribution approaches, and identifies discrepancies between last-click and multi-touch reporting. The strategist reviews the findings and determines which attribution approach provides the most useful and defensible representation of search marketing's contribution for the intended decision context. Based on the strategist's decisions, the agent updates the reporting dashboards and recalculates the relevant performance and investment metrics for the search portfolio. Finally, the strategist assesses the resulting performance evidence and presents the strategic insights to organizational leadership.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|attribution reasoning, financial alignment, cross-channel interpretation, business valuation, strategic reporting|data aggregation, model comparison, discrepancy identification, cohort analysis, dashboard generation|The AI agent performs systematic data aggregation and compares attribution approaches, while the strategist interprets the business validity of the evidence and determines the appropriate strategic reporting.|

### 1.4.8. Algorithm Adaptation and Search Trend Forecasting

Algorithm adaptation and search trend forecasting connects search marketing strategies with the continuous evolution of search engine systems and shifting search behavior. This responsibility includes monitoring search engine updates, identifying ranking volatility, analyzing emerging search trends, defining adaptation strategies, and adjusting the search roadmap as technical and market conditions change.

Agentic skills support SERP volatility tracking, update summarization, trend analysis, scenario comparison, and preparation of technical adaptation requirements. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the long-term strategic impact of search environment changes, explaining technical trade-offs to stakeholders, resolving conflicting priorities between SEO and product teams, and maintaining alignment between search trends and organizational capabilities.

For example, a major search engine first releases a core algorithm update that causes significant ranking volatility across the organization's primary digital assets. The search marketing strategist defines which content categories and technical signals require immediate investigation. An AI agent then tracks SERP volatility, compares affected and unaffected pages, examines available search engine guidance, and identifies patterns that warrant further investigation. The strategist reviews the evidence, discusses potential remediation trade-offs with content and engineering stakeholders, and determines which adaptations are justified without disrupting the core user experience. Based on the agreed direction, the agent updates the technical audit parameters and generates the content revision guidelines. Finally, the strategist communicates the revised adaptation roadmap and ensures that the search strategy remains aligned with the evolving search environment.

|algorithmic interpretation, trend analysis, stakeholder negotiation, adaptation planning, strategic alignment|volatility tracking, update summarization, scenario comparison, guideline generation, documentation support|The AI agent tracks search volatility and organizes evidence about emerging changes, while the strategist interprets their strategic significance and maintains alignment between search strategy and organizational capabilities.|
