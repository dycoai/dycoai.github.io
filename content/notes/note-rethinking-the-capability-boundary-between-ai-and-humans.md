Where should we draw the capability boundary between AI and humans? It may be tempting to assume that AI can never be as productive or innovative as a human, but this assumption is becoming increasingly difficult to maintain. Consider video generation models as an example. A carefully designed prompt or set of instructions is itself a product of human thinking, but the resulting video can sometimes be remarkably detailed, coherent, and visually innovative. It may therefore be too simple to attribute the quality of the final result entirely to the human contribution. Imagine two teams, each consisting of an instructor and a designer: one team works with a human designer, while the other works with a video generation model. Can we confidently assume that the human designer will always produce a better result? There may be situations in which the AI-assisted team performs just as well or even produces a result that the human team could not easily achieve.

The common response is that AI can only generate such results because its training data contains the accumulated creative work of many artists and designers. This is an important part of understanding how current AI systems work, but it also raises a broader question about human creativity. Individual artists also develop their abilities through accumulated experience, observation, education, and exposure to the work of others. Few people create without any form of learning from what came before them. Therefore, simply pointing to the human contributions contained in AI's training process does not completely settle the question of whether the resulting output can be considered innovative.

For me, this uncertainty is itself part of coping with AI. It may be unhelpful to overestimate human capabilities or to assume that AI is merely an extension of human ability. At the same time, there is no need to reach a final conclusion about whether AI is ultimately more creative or capable than humans. What matters more is discovering what these systems can actually do and learning how to use their capabilities to become more productive and capable ourselves. This also requires accepting that AI may eventually take responsibility for many tasks that are currently performed by people. Rather than defining our relationship with AI in terms of who must remain superior, I think it is more practical to observe its capabilities carefully, adapt our skills accordingly, and continue finding areas where human judgment and AI capabilities can be used together.
