# 1. Working with Agentic AI: Blockchain Engineer

How human skills and agentic skills are dynamically integrated and coordinated across blockchain engineering responsibilities

## 1.1. Blockchain Engineer as an Occupation
A blockchain engineer designs, implements, and secures distributed ledger protocols, consensus mechanisms, and smart contracts to build scalable, trustless, and decentralized systems. This occupation connects cryptography, distributed systems theory, game theory, and software engineering by transforming theoretical trust models and economic incentives into robust, immutable, and executable network architectures. To ensure that these decentralized systems operate securely and efficiently without central oversight, the engineer must understand how cryptographic primitives, network latency, state machine replication, economic attack vectors, and scalability constraints collectively determine the viability and security of the blockchain ecosystem.

Typical responsibilities of a blockchain engineer include:
* blockchain architecture and protocol design;
* smart contract development and security auditing;
* decentralized application (DApp) integration and backend engineering;
* cryptographic implementation and consensus optimization;
* network infrastructure, node operations, and scalability; and
* blockchain testing, deployment, and lifecycle management.

These responsibilities are fulfilled through distinct tasks and subtasks, each requiring one or more actions with specific skill requirements. Human skills and agentic skills both contribute to these responsibilities, but their levels and forms of involvement differ according to the responsibility, task, action, data, architectural context, technical environment, and expected outcome.

## 1.2. Human Skills and Agentic Skills in Blockchain Engineering

Blockchain engineering increasingly integrates both human skills and agentic skills.

Human skills are reusable capabilities possessed and developed by blockchain engineers. They include distributed systems reasoning, cryptographic intuition, game-theoretic modeling, security mindset, economic incentive design, threat modeling, state-machine logic formulation, and architectural judgment.

Agentic skills are reusable capabilities available to AI agents through their underlying models, contextual information, instructions, constraints, tools, and computational resources. In blockchain engineering, these skills include smart contract code generation, static vulnerability analysis, fuzzing script creation, gas optimization suggestion, formal verification scaffolding, network simulation, log parsing, and documentation drafting.

Both skill types contribute to the same occupational responsibilities, but their involvement varies by action. Understanding the economic implications of a novel tokenomics model or identifying a complex logical re-entrancy exploit relies primarily on human knowledge of game theory and adversarial thinking; an AI agent may then use agentic skills to generate thousands of fuzzing test cases, simulate network congestion, and scan the codebase for known vulnerability signatures. In protocol design, the engineer first determines the appropriate consensus mechanism and trust assumptions, and an AI agent subsequently scaffolds the peer-to-peer networking code and simulates message propagation latencies. Across these actions, human and agentic skills are activated sequentially or iteratively based on task requirements, with the human professional maintaining oversight and final judgment.

The relationship between these skills is not a fixed division where certain responsibilities permanently belong to humans and others to AI. Instead, human and agentic involvement shifts across tasks and between different actions within the same task.

## 1.3. Dynamic Human–Agentic Skill Integration and Collaboration in Blockchain Engineering

Blockchain engineering functions can be understood as an occupation where responsibilities are fulfilled through tasks and subtasks, tasks and subtasks are executed through actions, and actions require appropriate configurations of human and agentic skills.

An occupation provides the overall professional context. For blockchain engineering, this context encompasses designing decentralized protocols, writing and securing immutable smart contracts, optimizing network consensus, and integrating distributed ledgers with external systems.

A responsibility represents an expected area of professional work or an outcome that the blockchain engineer accomplishes or maintains. Responsibilities such as architecture design, smart contract auditing, and network scalability each contain multiple tasks.

A task is a context-dependent unit of work used to fulfill part or all of a responsibility. When a task is too broad or complex for reliable execution, it is decomposed into smaller subtasks.

Tasks and subtasks are executed through one or more actions. Actions represent reusable and configurable operations such as drafting a consensus algorithm, writing a state-transition function, executing a static analysis tool, generating a zero-knowledge proof circuit, simulating a network partition, or formatting an upgrade proposal.

Each action has skill requirements. These requirements describe the capabilities needed for effective and reliable execution. Depending on the action and its context, human skills, agentic skills, or an integration of both can satisfy these requirements.

The skills available for blockchain engineering reside in a skill space containing both human skills and agentic skills. Relevant skills in this space can serve as candidate skills for executing the actions required by a particular task. A human skill in adversarial threat modeling, for example, can serve as a candidate for identifying economic exploits in a decentralized exchange protocol, while an agentic skill in automated fuzzing can serve as a candidate for generating the edge-case transaction sequences required to test that model.

Candidate skills are evaluated relative to the requirements of the action and its occupational and task context. Appropriate skills are then selected and organized into a skill configuration for execution. Depending on these requirements, a configuration may rely mainly on human skills, mainly on agentic skills, or on an integrated arrangement of both.

### 1.3.1. Skills Competition and Skills Collaboration

Two important relationships can occur between human skills and agentic skills when constructing a skill configuration: skills competition and skills collaboration.

Skills competition occurs when both a human skill and an agentic skill can satisfy the same or similar requirements for an action, but simultaneous application of both is unnecessary. Under a particular occupational and task context, one skill type can provide a better fit for the action than the other.

For example, both a blockchain engineer and an AI agent can generate a standard summary of gas consumption across a suite of smart contract functions. When the bytecode and optimization targets are clearly specified, the agentic skill provides fast and scalable execution. When the summary requires interpreting why a specific state-update logic is fundamentally inefficient due to underlying virtual machine architecture constraints, the engineer's human skills provide the necessary systemic understanding.

The purpose of skills competition is not to establish whether human or agentic skills are generally superior. Rather, it identifies which available skill provides the better fit for a particular action under particular conditions.

Skills collaboration occurs when one skill type alone cannot effectively satisfy the requirements of a task or its actions, or when combining complementary human and agentic skills produces stronger task performance.

Collaboration does not require human and agentic skills to operate simultaneously. Instead, they are activated at different stages of action execution in a sequential, iterative, or recursive manner according to evolving task requirements.

For example, an engineer first designs a novel decentralized voting mechanism and defines the required cryptographic constraints and economic penalties for malicious behavior. An AI agent then generates the initial smart contract code, scaffolds the zero-knowledge proof circuits, and runs automated static analysis to check for standard vulnerabilities. The engineer subsequently reviews the code and identifies a subtle logic flaw where a specific sequence of network delays could allow a minority of validators to censor votes without triggering the economic penalties. Based on the engineer's direction, the agent next rewrites the state-transition logic and generates new formal verification proofs. Finally, the engineer evaluates the resulting architecture to ensure it is mathematically sound and economically secure. Throughout this sequence, human and agentic skills are activated iteratively as task requirements evolve, while the engineer directs the investigation and evaluates its outcomes.

This dynamic relationship allows blockchain engineers to leverage the complementary strengths of both skill types rather than relying on a permanent allocation of work.

### 1.3.2. Skill Configuration and Task Execution

A skill configuration specifies which human skills and agentic skills are used to execute the actions associated with a task or subtask and how those skills are coordinated.

The configuration depends on factors such as action requirements, security criticality, immutability constraints, computational complexity, expected reliability, available cryptographic libraries, network topology, decision consequences, and intermediate results.

For one action, an agentic skill may independently satisfy the requirements. For another, a human skill may provide the better fit. A complex task may require several configurations across multiple actions, with human and agentic skills repeatedly interacting as the task progresses.

The configuration is therefore dynamic rather than permanent. It may change when the action changes, new vulnerability disclosures are published, an agentic result fails to satisfy expected security thresholds, or human interpretation identifies a new economic attack vector.

Throughout this process, the blockchain engineer remains the professional responsible for directing and controlling the work associated with the occupation and its responsibilities. Agentic AI provides powerful complementary capabilities that expand what the professional can simulate, generate, test, analyze, optimize, and document.

### 1.3.3. Task Performance Evaluation and Skill Space Adaptation

The performance produced through a selected skill configuration is evaluated against predefined evaluation metrics. These metrics determine whether the human skills, agentic skills, or their integrated configuration are sufficiently capable of performing the relevant task and its required actions.

Evaluation criteria depend on the work being performed. In blockchain engineering, they include cryptographic soundness, code immutability, gas efficiency, vulnerability resistance, consensus liveness, network throughput, reproducibility, and adherence to protocol specifications.

When a selected skill or skill configuration produces performance above the defined threshold, it passes the performance evaluation and is retained for future applications under appropriate conditions. Repeated successful use provides additional evidence about where that skill or configuration performs effectively.

When performance falls below the required threshold, the corresponding skill or configuration is not reused without modification. The relevant human or agentic skills may be refined or reconfigured, existing skills can be combined differently, or new human and agentic skills may be developed or constructed from scratch when the existing skill space lacks adequate capability.

For example, an AI agent first generates smart contract code that passes all standard unit tests and static analysis checks, but repeatedly produces logic that is vulnerable to complex flash-loan economic exploits. Task performance evaluation identifies this security gap as falling below the required threshold. The agentic skill is then refined through the integration of specialized economic constraint prompts, adversarial simulation tools, or access to updated vulnerability databases. The revised skill is evaluated again, and once it satisfies the required performance criteria, it is retained for appropriate future development tasks. Human skills follow a corresponding development process: when an engineer lacks the mathematical background required to implement a newly published post-quantum cryptographic signature scheme, the relevant capability is developed through studying the literature and experimenting with test vectors before its performance is evaluated through subsequent protocol integrations.

Refined and newly constructed skills that satisfy the required performance criteria are added to the skill space. In this way, task performance evaluation provides feedback not only for completing current work but also for continuously optimizing and expanding the available skill space.

The skill space is not constructed as an isolated collection of occupation-specific representations. Many human and agentic skills used in blockchain engineering are reusable across occupations. Distributed systems reasoning, code generation, vulnerability scanning, cryptographic implementation, network simulation, and documentation contribute to work in cybersecurity, backend engineering, cloud architecture, data engineering, and many other fields.

Reusable skills can therefore be represented once and configured differently according to the responsibilities, tasks, actions, and contexts of different occupations. This allows the skill space to serve a growing range of occupations without unnecessarily duplicating or exploding the number of skill representations. Occupational specialization is introduced through task context, action requirements, parameters, data sources, tools, constraints, and skill configurations rather than by creating a completely separate skill representation whenever the same underlying capability appears in another occupation.

The resulting process is adaptive:
occupational responsibility → task and subtask → actions → skill requirements → candidate human and agentic skills → skill configuration → task execution → task performance evaluation → skill refinement, reconfiguration, or construction → optimized and expanded skill space

This cycle allows human and agentic capabilities to evolve alongside the requirements of blockchain engineering work.

## 1.4. Essential Responsibilities of Blockchain Engineer

### 1.4.1. Blockchain Architecture and Protocol Design

Blockchain architecture and protocol design establishes the foundational rules, data structures, and trust models of a distributed ledger. This responsibility includes defining the state machine, selecting consensus algorithms, designing tokenomics and incentive structures, specifying peer-to-peer networking protocols, and documenting the technical architecture for development teams and external auditors.

Agentic skills contribute extensively to literature retrieval on consensus mechanisms, drafting architecture documentation, simulating network topologies, and generating boilerplate networking code. Human skills remain essential for applying game theory to prevent validator centralization, designing economic incentives that align network participants, resolving tradeoffs between decentralization and throughput (the blockchain trilemma), and determining the ultimate security assumptions of the protocol.

For example, a team initiates the design of a new Layer-1 blockchain optimized for high-frequency microtransactions. The blockchain engineer defines the required trust assumptions and the economic penalties for validator downtime. An AI agent then researches existing consensus variants, generates comparative latency simulations, and drafts the initial peer-to-peer message schemas. The engineer reviews the simulations and identifies that the proposed block propagation mechanism would inadvertently favor validators with specialized hardware, threatening network decentralization. The engineer revises the protocol rules to introduce randomized committee selection, and the agent updates the architecture documentation and simulation parameters accordingly.

| Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
| ---|---|---|
| distributed systems reasoning, game-theoretic modeling, incentive design, trilemma tradeoff evaluation|literature retrieval, network simulation, schema generation, documentation drafting|The engineer defines the economic and security constraints of the trust model, while the AI agent accelerates the simulation, coding, and documentation of the protocol architecture.|

### 1.4.2. Smart Contract Development and Security Auditing

Smart contract development and security auditing involves writing, testing, and securing the immutable code that executes business logic on the blockchain. This responsibility includes designing state-transition logic, writing code in specialized languages, optimizing gas consumption, conducting internal security audits, and preparing code for external formal verification.

Agentic skills are well suited to smart contract code scaffolding, static vulnerability scanning, automated fuzzing script generation, gas optimization suggestions, and unit test creation. Human skills contribute to threat modeling, identifying complex economic exploits (such as flash-loan attacks or oracle manipulation), verifying that the code accurately reflects the intended business logic, and making architectural decisions that minimize the attack surface.

For example, an engineer is developing a decentralized lending protocol. An AI agent generates the initial smart contract code based on the engineer's logic specifications, runs static analysis to catch standard re-entrancy flaws, and suggests gas-optimized variable packing. The engineer reviews the code and realizes that while the math is correct, the liquidation logic could be manipulated by an attacker artificially inflating the price of a low-liquidity collateral asset via a decentralized exchange. The engineer redesigns the oracle integration to use time-weighted average prices (TWAP), and the agent regenerates the affected code modules and corresponding test cases to verify the new security boundary.

| Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
| ---|---|---|
| threat modeling, economic exploit prevention, business logic verification, attack surface reduction|code scaffolding, static analysis, fuzzing generation, gas optimization, unit test creation|The AI agent scales the coding, standard vulnerability scanning, and optimization, while the engineer identifies complex economic attack vectors and governs the overarching security architecture.|

### 1.4.3. Decentralized Application (DApp) Integration and Backend Engineering

Decentralized application (DApp) integration and backend engineering connects on-chain smart contracts with off-chain user interfaces, databases, and external data sources. This responsibility includes designing event-indexing architectures, managing remote procedure call (RPC) node connections, integrating decentralized storage solutions, and ensuring data consistency between the immutable ledger and traditional backend systems.

Agentic skills assist with API endpoint generation, RPC load-balancing scripts, event-listener scaffolding, and automated indexing pipeline configuration. Human skills remain important for designing the state management strategy, handling network reorganizations (reorgs) gracefully, evaluating the trust assumptions of off-chain data oracles, and ensuring the user experience does not compromise the underlying decentralization guarantees.

For example, an organization is building a supply chain tracking DApp. The engineer defines the strategy for indexing millions of on-chain transfer events into a traditional queryable database. An AI agent generates the event-listener scripts, configures the indexing pipeline, and writes the API endpoints for the frontend. The engineer reviews the architecture and identifies that the current setup will fail to handle deep blockchain reorganizations, potentially serving users false historical data. The engineer instructs the agent to implement a reorg-handling middleware that delays database finality until a safe block depth is reached, and the agent updates the integration code to reflect this constraint.

| Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
| ---|---|---|
| state management design, reorg handling, oracle trust evaluation, decentralization preservation|API generation, event-listener scaffolding, indexing configuration, RPC scripting|The AI agent rapidly builds the off-chain integration and indexing pipelines, while the engineer ensures the architecture gracefully handles distributed network anomalies and preserves data integrity.|

### 1.4.4. Cryptographic Implementation and Consensus Optimization

Cryptographic implementation and consensus optimization applies advanced mathematical primitives to secure network communications, validate transactions, and optimize the performance of the consensus mechanism. This responsibility includes selecting hash functions and signature schemes, implementing zero-knowledge proof circuits, tuning consensus parameters for latency and throughput, and managing cryptographic key lifecycles.

Agentic skills retrieve cryptographic library documentation, generate test vectors for new primitives, benchmark hashing algorithms, and scaffold zero-knowledge circuit logic. Human skills evaluate the long-term security of cryptographic assumptions, analyze the potential impact of quantum computing on current primitives, tune consensus parameters to prevent network partitioning, and ensure that cryptographic implementations do not introduce side-channel vulnerabilities.

For example, a protocol requires the integration of a new zero-knowledge proof system to enable private transactions. The engineer evaluates the mathematical trust setup requirements and selects a specific proving system that balances proof size with generation speed. An AI agent then scaffolds the circuit logic, generates thousands of test vectors to verify edge cases, and benchmarks the compilation time. The engineer reviews the benchmarks and determines that the proof generation latency is too high for mobile wallet integration. The engineer revises the circuit constraints to batch multiple transactions into a single proof, and the agent re-runs the benchmarks to confirm the optimization meets the usability threshold.

| Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
| ---|---|---|
| cryptographic assumption evaluation, quantum readiness analysis, side-channel prevention, parameter tuning|library retrieval, test vector generation, benchmarking, circuit scaffolding|The AI agent handles the computationally intensive benchmarking and circuit scaffolding, while the engineer governs the mathematical trust assumptions and optimizes the cryptographic architecture for real-world constraints.|

### 1.4.5. Network Infrastructure, Node Operations, and Scalability

Network infrastructure, node operations, and scalability ensures that the distributed ledger remains highly available, resilient to network partitions, and capable of scaling to meet transaction demand. This responsibility includes designing sharding or Layer-2 scaling solutions, configuring node deployment environments, monitoring peer-to-peer network health, and diagnosing consensus bottlenecks.

Agentic skills contribute to automated node provisioning, continuous log parsing, mempool monitoring, latency alerting, and the generation of infrastructure-as-code templates. Human skills define the geographic and jurisdictional distribution requirements for node operators to prevent censorship, diagnose complex network-level denial-of-service attacks, and design the economic and technical parameters of Layer-2 rollups or sidechains.

For example, a blockchain network experiences intermittent consensus halts during periods of peak transaction volume. An AI agent continuously parses node logs, monitors mempool sizes, and identifies that the halts correlate with a specific type of complex smart contract execution that exhausts node memory. The agent drafts a proposed patch to limit the computational complexity of specific opcodes. The blockchain engineer reviews the diagnostic data, confirms the agent's hypothesis, and evaluates the economic impact of restricting those opcodes on existing DApp developers. The engineer authorizes the patch and instructs the agent to generate the network upgrade proposal and migration documentation.

| Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
| ---|---|---|
| censorship resistance design, network attack diagnosis, scalability architecture, upgrade governance|node provisioning, log parsing, mempool monitoring, infrastructure templating|The AI agent scales the monitoring, log analysis, and infrastructure deployment, while the engineer diagnoses systemic network bottlenecks and governs the architectural scaling solutions.|

### 1.4.6. Blockchain Testing, Deployment, and Lifecycle Management

Blockchain testing, deployment, and lifecycle management governs the safe release of protocol upgrades, smart contract migrations, and network forks. This responsibility includes managing testnet environments, coordinating multi-signature deployment transactions, executing state-migration scripts during hard forks, and establishing emergency response protocols for post-deployment vulnerabilities.

Agentic skills assist with testnet deployment automation, generating state-migration scripts, monitoring deployment transactions, and drafting incident response runbooks. Human skills evaluate the systemic risks of a hard fork, coordinate the social consensus required among node operators and validators, manage emergency pause mechanisms during active exploits, and make the final authorization to deploy immutable code to the mainnet.

For example, a major protocol upgrade requires migrating millions of user states from an old smart contract architecture to a new one. The engineer defines the exact mathematical mapping required to preserve user balances and governance rights during the migration. An AI agent generates the state-migration script, tests it against a forked mainnet environment, and drafts the step-by-step deployment runbook. The engineer reviews the test results, identifies a critical edge case involving delegated voting power that the agent's script failed to map correctly, and manually corrects the migration logic. Once verified, the engineer coordinates the social consensus for the upgrade block height and authorizes the final deployment.

| Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
| ---|---|---|
| fork risk evaluation, social consensus coordination, emergency response, deployment authorization|testnet automation, migration scripting, deployment monitoring, runbook drafting|The AI agent automates the testing and scripting of complex state migrations, while the engineer evaluates the systemic risks, corrects critical edge cases, and authorizes the immutable mainnet deployment.|

## 1.5. Changing Human and Agentic Involvement Across Blockchain Engineer Responsibilities

Human skills and agentic skills are involved throughout blockchain engineering, but their relative involvement differs across responsibilities and changes during task execution.

| Responsibility|Typical human involvement|Typical agentic involvement|Typical integration|
| ---|---|---|---|
| Blockchain architecture and protocol design|Very high|High|Agentic network simulation and documentation combined with human game-theoretic modeling and incentive design.|
| Smart contract development and security auditing|Very high|Very high|Agentic code scaffolding and static analysis governed by human threat modeling and economic exploit prevention.|
| Decentralized application (DApp) integration and backend engineering|High|Very high|Agentic API generation and indexing configuration combined with human state management design and reorg handling.|
| Cryptographic implementation and consensus optimization|Very high|High|Agentic benchmarking and circuit scaffolding supporting human cryptographic assumption evaluation and parameter tuning.|
| Network infrastructure, node operations, and scalability|High|Very high|Agentic log parsing and node provisioning governed by human network attack diagnosis and scalability architecture.|
| Blockchain testing, deployment, and lifecycle management|Very high|High|Agentic migration scripting and testnet automation combined with human fork risk evaluation and deployment authorization.|

These involvement levels are not permanent allocations. A single responsibility may contain actions where human and agentic skills compete as alternative capabilities and other actions where they collaborate sequentially, iteratively, or recursively.

The practical objective is to construct skill configurations that use the most suitable available capabilities for each part of the work and revise those configurations when task performance evaluation shows that another arrangement performs more effectively or reliably.

## 1.6. Working as a Blockchain Engineer with Agentic AI

Agentic AI is becoming an increasingly powerful component of blockchain engineering. Blockchain engineers should therefore adopt and integrate AI agents as complementary participants in professional workflows rather than treating them only as occasional tools for generating smart contract boilerplate, parsing node logs, or drafting documentation.

This shift does not reduce the importance of cryptographic, economic, and distributed systems expertise. Human professionals remain responsible for the occupation and its corresponding responsibilities. They define the trust assumptions, design the economic incentives, evaluate the security boundaries, interpret network anomalies, communicate with protocol stakeholders, control consequential mainnet deployments, evaluate task performance, and decide how human and agentic skills are configured and improved.

At the same time, agentic skills greatly expand the volume and complexity of engineering work that can be executed. AI agents retrieve and organize vast amounts of cryptographic literature, generate and test smart contract code, simulate network topologies, scan for static vulnerabilities, benchmark zero-knowledge circuits, automate node deployments, and synthesize extensive technical documentation at a scale difficult to achieve through direct human effort alone.

Strong blockchain engineering practice is therefore not based on maximizing human work or agentic work independently. It is based on finding appropriate configurations of human skills and agentic skills for the responsibilities, tasks, subtasks, and actions being performed.

Where a human skill and an agentic skill can independently perform the same action, skills competition identifies which is better suited to the current context. Where work requires complementary capabilities, skills collaboration results in human and agentic skills being activated sequentially, iteratively, or recursively to achieve stronger task performance than either skill type achieves alone.

Task performance evaluation then determines whether selected skills and configurations satisfy predefined expectations. Successful skills are retained and reused, while inadequate skills are refined, reconfigured, or newly developed. The resulting capabilities return to an increasingly useful skill space that supports blockchain engineering and, where capabilities are reusable, other occupations as well.

For a blockchain engineer, working effectively with agentic AI means treating AI as a powerful complementary capability integrated into everyday professional routines while maintaining human control of occupational responsibilities. Through appropriate skill selection, competition, collaboration, evaluation, and continuous improvement, human professionals and agentic AI work together to achieve blockchain systems that are more efficient, scalable, adaptable, timely, cryptographically sound, and fundamentally secure.
