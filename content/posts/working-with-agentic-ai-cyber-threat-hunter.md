# 1. Working with Agentic AI: Cyber Threat Hunter

*How Human Skills and Agentic Skills Dynamically Combine Across Cyber Threat Hunting Responsibilities*

## 1.1. Cyber Threat Hunting as Professional Work

A **cyber threat hunter** proactively searches systems, networks, endpoints, cloud environments, identities, applications, security telemetry, and other authorized data sources for evidence of malicious activity that has not been adequately identified by existing automated security controls. Rather than depending entirely on predefined alerts, the role involves developing hunt hypotheses, investigating anomalous behavior, analyzing security telemetry, examining adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures, correlating evidence across multiple sources, identifying indicators of compromise or suspicious behavior, investigating possible attacker persistence and lateral movement, distinguishing malicious activity from legitimate behavior, and determining whether suspicious findings warrant escalation. Cyber threat hunters also contribute to threat intelligence application, detection engineering, incident investigation, security-control improvement, threat-hunting methodology, documentation, reporting, and collaboration with security operations, incident response, digital forensics, vulnerability management, security engineering, and other cybersecurity functions. Their work therefore combines proactive investigation, adversary understanding, technical analysis, contextual reasoning, and continuous improvement of defensive capabilities.

## 1.2. Working with Agentic AI

**Agentic AI** can participate across cyber threat hunting by applying language comprehension, reasoning, classification, information retrieval, coding, pattern analysis, comparison, generation, evaluation, and workflow-execution capabilities to security telemetry, endpoint events, network records, authentication activity, cloud logs, threat intelligence, detection rules, incident histories, vulnerability information, system inventories, and other authorized cybersecurity sources. An AI agent can help collect and normalize hunt-relevant information, generate preliminary hunt hypotheses, translate hypotheses into search strategies, construct or refine queries, correlate events across multiple data sources, identify unusual behavioral patterns, compare observed activity with known adversary techniques, retrieve relevant threat intelligence, summarize suspicious activity, organize investigation evidence, generate preliminary timelines, identify information gaps, prepare hunt reports, and convert validated findings into candidate detection improvements. These activities can operate as interconnected workflows in which the agent retrieves evidence, executes authorized analytical tools, evaluates intermediate findings, revises queries, expands or narrows investigative scope, and adjusts subsequent actions when new telemetry, intelligence, analyst judgments, or investigation results become available. The cyber threat hunter remains important for developing meaningful investigative hypotheses, understanding environmental context, distinguishing legitimate but unusual activity from adversary behavior, evaluating uncertain evidence, recognizing novel attack patterns, directing deeper investigation, determining the significance of findings, and making or escalating consequential cybersecurity decisions.

## 1.3. Human Skills and Agentic Skills

**Human skills** in cyber threat hunting include adversarial thinking, cybersecurity knowledge, investigative reasoning, hypothesis development, technical intuition, contextual interpretation, anomaly evaluation, evidence assessment, creativity, persistence, risk judgment, and the ability to understand how apparently unrelated activities across endpoints, identities, applications, networks, and cloud resources may form a meaningful pattern of adversary behavior. These skills are especially important when malicious activity deliberately resembles legitimate administration, evidence is incomplete or distributed across multiple systems, normal behavior differs among users or assets, known indicators are unavailable, or an adversary changes techniques to evade existing detections. The cyber threat hunter also performs analytical activities that can overlap substantially with AI capabilities, including log analysis, information retrieval, event correlation, threat intelligence analysis, query development, pattern recognition, evidence organization, and investigation documentation.

**Agentic skills** in cyber threat hunting are task-oriented capabilities through which an AI agent can assist with hypothesis generation, telemetry analysis, threat intelligence retrieval, behavioral comparison, event correlation, query development, anomaly investigation, evidence organization, hunt documentation, and detection improvement. Depending on available systems and authorization, these skills may retrieve and normalize security data, generate search queries or analytical code, compare activity across hosts and accounts, correlate events over time, identify behavioral outliers, map observed activity to known adversary techniques, retrieve related intelligence, organize investigative timelines, classify findings, summarize evidence, maintain hunt status, and identify possible gaps in existing detections. Their usefulness depends on telemetry coverage, data quality, environmental context, system access, model capability, tool availability, adversary sophistication, false-positive rates, and the level of reliability required for the investigation.

The relationship between human skills and agentic skills in cyber threat hunting should therefore be understood as a **dynamic and collaborative division of work between the cyber threat hunter and Agentic AI across different professional responsibilities**. The cyber threat hunter contributes human skills such as adversarial thinking, investigative judgment, hypothesis formation, contextual interpretation, technical intuition, uncertainty evaluation, and risk judgment when performing responsibilities including hunt planning, behavioral analysis, anomaly investigation, adversary tracking, compromise assessment, evidence evaluation, and escalation. Agentic AI contributes agentic skills such as high-volume telemetry processing, information retrieval, query generation, event correlation, pattern comparison, anomaly identification, intelligence retrieval, evidence organization, and iterative analytical support across many of the same responsibilities. The relative contribution of each can change continuously according to the hunt hypothesis, available telemetry, environmental conditions, adversary behavior, strength of evidence, investigative progress, and potential consequences of the findings. A hunt may therefore involve human-generated hypotheses followed by agentic analysis, agent-generated investigative leads followed by human validation, or repeated exchanges in which findings from one side reshape the next analytical action of the other. The practical objective is not to assign complete hunts permanently to either the human analyst or Agentic AI, but to **dynamically combine and coordinate the human skills of the cyber threat hunter with the agentic skills of Agentic AI according to the changing requirements of each responsibility, task, and subtask**.

## 1.4. Dynamic Allocation of Tasks and Subtasks

A responsibility such as hypothesis-driven hunting, telemetry analysis, behavioral investigation, or compromise assessment consists of multiple tasks and subtasks rather than one indivisible activity. These subtasks can require different combinations of information retrieval, query development, data analysis, event correlation, adversarial reasoning, contextual interpretation, evidence evaluation, and decision-making. Human and agentic participation can therefore vary within the same threat-hunting responsibility.

The allocation of work should be understood as **dynamic rather than fixed**. An agent may initially analyze large volumes of telemetry, identify an unusual behavioral pattern, escalate the finding to the cyber threat hunter for contextual interpretation, receive a revised hypothesis or investigative direction after human review, and then continue searching additional systems or data sources. Other analytical subtasks may be completed largely through agentic skills when the data, investigative objective, tools, and authorization are sufficiently clear. Allocation can change according to adversary sophistication, data quality, environmental complexity, investigation consequence, model capability, tool access, output reliability, false-positive rates, intermediate findings, and organizational security controls.

A useful way to understand this structure is:

**Occupation → Responsibility → Task → Subtask → Human and/or Agentic Skills → Evaluation → Possible Reallocation**

The purpose of integrating agentic skills is therefore not to remove professional investigative responsibility. It is to expand the analytical capabilities available to the cyber threat hunter and enable work to be allocated according to the evolving requirements of each hunt.

## 1.7. Why the Division of Work Changes

The boundary between human and agentic participation can change from one hunt, hypothesis, suspicious event, investigative lead, task, or subtask to another.

| Allocation Factor                | Effect on Human–Agentic Work                                 |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Investigation complexity**     | Complex hunts involving multiple systems, identities, techniques, or incomplete attack paths may require greater human investigative reasoning and iterative agentic analysis. |
| **Potential consequence**        | Findings suggesting active compromise, privileged access, data loss, destructive activity, or other significant threats require stronger human validation and escalation judgment. |
| **Telemetry availability**       | Agentic analysis depends on sufficiently complete, relevant, timely, and authorized endpoint, network, identity, cloud, application, and security telemetry. |
| **Data quality**                 | Incomplete, inconsistent, noisy, incorrectly normalized, or poorly contextualized security data can reduce analytical reliability and increase human review requirements. |
| **Model capability**             | More capable models may perform increasingly sophisticated correlation, query generation, evidence analysis, and investigative reasoning. |
| **Agentic skill availability**   | Specialized skills for log analysis, threat intelligence, behavioral analytics, query development, evidence correlation, and other hunting activities determine what can be delegated effectively. |
| **Tool access**                  | Access to SIEM, endpoint telemetry, network monitoring, cloud logs, security analytics, threat intelligence, scripting environments, and other authorized tools expands agentic capability. |
| **Output reliability**           | Unsupported correlations, weak hypotheses, false positives, or technically incorrect interpretations require human validation or reassignment. |
| **Intermediate findings**        | New indicators, anomalous behavior, compromised accounts, suspicious hosts, or additional intelligence can immediately change hunt scope and investigative priorities. |
| **Adversary sophistication**     | Attackers using novel, evasive, low-and-slow, or legitimate-tool-based techniques may require greater human creativity, contextual reasoning, and adversarial thinking. |
| **Environmental context**        | Normal behavior differs among organizations, systems, users, applications, and infrastructure, making contextual understanding important for interpreting anomalies. |
| **Authority and accountability** | AI capability does not itself provide authority to declare compromise, initiate consequential containment, authorize disruptive actions, or make final risk decisions. |

These factors can operate simultaneously. A high-volume telemetry-analysis task may be largely agentic under well-understood conditions, while a subtle behavioral anomaly associated with a potentially sophisticated adversary may immediately shift the investigation toward human-led reasoning and repeated human–agentic collaboration.

## 1.8. Developing Cyber Threat Hunting Capability with Agentic AI

The increasing availability of agentic skills does not eliminate the need for traditional threat-hunting expertise. Adversarial thinking, cybersecurity knowledge, technical intuition, hypothesis development, investigative reasoning, contextual interpretation, evidence evaluation, creativity, persistence, and risk judgment remain important because they allow the cyber threat hunter to determine what should be investigated, recognize when apparently legitimate activity may conceal malicious behavior, evaluate uncertain evidence, and determine whether findings represent meaningful security threats.

Professional capability increasingly also includes the ability to delegate suitable analytical subtasks, provide appropriate investigative context, connect agents with authorized telemetry and tools, evaluate generated hypotheses and queries, recognize unsupported correlations, request deeper analysis, redirect investigations, combine evidence from multiple agentic activities, and determine when direct human or specialist intervention is necessary. Competence therefore includes not only performing hunts directly but also **orchestrating how investigative work should be distributed across available human and agentic capabilities**.

This creates a broader model of professional capability:

**Professional Capability = Human Skills + Agentic Skills + Human–Agentic Coordination**

The proportions of these components will differ across hunts and responsibilities and will continue to change as models, adversary techniques, security telemetry, analytical tools, defensive technologies, and cybersecurity practices evolve.

## 1.9. The Evolving Boundary Between Human and Agentic Skills

The relationship between a **cyber threat hunter and Agentic AI is best understood as an adaptive division of work rather than a fixed separation between human tasks and AI tasks**. Agentic skills may perform substantial portions of telemetry processing, log analysis, threat intelligence retrieval, event correlation, behavioral comparison, query generation, evidence organization, indicator investigation, documentation, and continuous analytical monitoring. Human participation becomes especially important when hunting requires novel hypotheses, adversarial creativity, interpretation of environment-specific behavior, reconstruction from incomplete evidence, evaluation of competing explanations, assessment of uncertain findings, or consequential decisions about compromise and escalation.

The resulting arrangement is therefore an **adaptive human–agentic workflow**. A cyber threat hunter may formulate an initial hypothesis, AI may translate it into multiple searches and analyze large volumes of telemetry, the hunter may interpret an unexpected behavioral pattern, AI may then correlate that pattern across additional systems and identities, and the hunter may subsequently determine whether the accumulated evidence justifies escalation or another investigative direction. The allocation can change repeatedly as new telemetry is retrieved, hypotheses are rejected, anomalous behavior is discovered, threat intelligence changes, additional assets become relevant, or evidence increases or decreases confidence in a suspected compromise.

The long-term value of Agentic AI in cyber threat hunting therefore lies not only in accelerating searches through security data. It lies in expanding the investigative capabilities available to the cyber threat hunter and enabling them to be **dynamically selected, combined, evaluated, and reallocated according to changing threat-hunting requirements**. This can improve telemetry coverage, analytical scale, investigative continuity, evidence correlation, hypothesis exploration, detection improvement, and responsiveness while preserving the adversarial reasoning, contextual understanding, and professional judgment required to identify sophisticated threats that automated defenses may otherwise fail to surface.
