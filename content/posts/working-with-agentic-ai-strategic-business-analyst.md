# 1. Working with Agentic AI: Strategic Business Analyst

_How human skills and agentic skills can be dynamically integrated and coordinated across strategic business analysis responsibilities_

## 1.1. Strategic Business Analyst as an Occupation

A **strategic business analyst** is a professional who analyzes organizational needs, business performance, processes, capabilities, stakeholder requirements, and potential solutions to support strategic decisions and organizational change. The occupation connects business strategy with operational analysis, data analysis, process improvement, solution development, technology-enabled change, and stakeholder decision-making.

Strategic business analysis extends beyond documenting requirements for individual systems or projects. A strategic business analyst must understand how organizational objectives, business capabilities, operational processes, stakeholder needs, information, technology, costs, risks, and expected benefits interact. The analyst helps transform broad problems and opportunities into structured business needs, alternative solutions, actionable requirements, and evidence-supported recommendations.

Typical responsibilities of a strategic business analyst include:

- strategic needs, opportunities, and current-state analysis;
- stakeholder engagement and requirements analysis;
- business process analysis, architecture, and improvement;
- business data, performance, and decision-support analysis;
- business case development and solution evaluation;
- solution validation, implementation readiness, and change support; and
- executive communication, documentation, and strategic advisory support.

These responsibilities are fulfilled through different tasks and subtasks, and each task may require multiple actions with different skill requirements. Human skills and agentic skills can both contribute to performing these responsibilities, but their levels and forms of involvement may differ according to the strategic importance of the issue, task ambiguity, stakeholder diversity, information quality, analytical complexity, organizational consequences, and expected outcome.

## 1.2. Human Skills and Agentic Skills in Strategic Business Analysis

Strategic business analysis increasingly involves both **human skills** and **agentic skills**.

Human skills are reusable capabilities possessed and developed by strategic business analysts. They may include strategic reasoning, business analysis, systems thinking, stakeholder engagement, requirements elicitation, process analysis, financial reasoning, problem solving, facilitation, communication, organizational understanding, negotiation, contextual interpretation, and professional judgment.

Agentic skills are reusable capabilities available to AI agents through their underlying models, contextual information, instructions, constraints, tools, and computational resources. In strategic business analysis, these skills may include information retrieval, document extraction, classification, summarization, comparison, data analysis, visualization generation, process-model generation, requirements drafting, gap analysis, scenario analysis, business-case support, consistency checking, report generation, and tool use.

Both skill types can contribute to the same occupational responsibilities, but their involvement levels vary. Strategic problem definition may depend heavily on human interpretation of organizational objectives and competing stakeholder interests while using agentic skills for research and evidence synthesis. Business process analysis may involve substantial agentic support for documentation and comparison while relying on human skills to understand why processes operate as they do. Business-case development may combine agentic financial and scenario analysis with human evaluation of strategic value, organizational feasibility, and risk.

The useful relationship is therefore not a fixed division in which certain responsibilities permanently belong to humans and others belong to AI. Human and agentic involvement can change from one task to another and even between different actions within the same task.

## 1.3. Dynamic Human–Agentic Skill Integration and Collaboration in Strategic Business Analysis

Strategic business analysis can be understood as an occupation in which responsibilities are fulfilled through tasks and subtasks, tasks and subtasks are executed through actions, and actions require appropriate configurations of human and agentic skills.

An **occupation** provides the overall professional context. For strategic business analysis, this context includes understanding organizational needs, identifying opportunities for improvement or change, analyzing business and operational conditions, evaluating possible solutions, and helping stakeholders make informed decisions.

A **responsibility** represents an expected area of professional work or an outcome that the strategic business analyst is expected to accomplish or maintain. Responsibilities such as strategic analysis, stakeholder requirements analysis, process improvement, or solution evaluation may each contain multiple tasks.

A **task** is a context-dependent unit of work used to fulfill part or all of a responsibility. When a task is too broad or complex for reliable execution, it may be decomposed into smaller **subtasks**.

Tasks and subtasks are executed through one or more **actions**. Actions represent reusable and configurable operations such as retrieving information, interviewing stakeholders, extracting requirements, comparing alternatives, mapping a process, analyzing data, calculating financial implications, identifying gaps, validating a solution, or preparing an executive recommendation.

Each action has **skill requirements**. These requirements describe the capabilities needed for the action to be executed effectively and reliably. Depending on the action and its context, the requirements may be satisfied by human skills, agentic skills, or an integration of both.

The skills available for strategic business analysis reside in a **skill space** containing both human skills and agentic skills. Some skills in this space become candidate skills for executing the actions required by a particular task. A human skill in stakeholder facilitation, for example, may be a candidate for resolving conflicting business needs, while an agentic skill in document comparison may be another candidate for identifying differences among stakeholder requirements.

The candidate skills are considered relative to the requirements of the action and the occupational and task context. Appropriate skills are then selected and organized into a **skill configuration** for execution. A configuration may rely mainly on human skills, mainly on agentic skills, or on an integrated arrangement of both.

### 1.3.1. Skills Competition and Skills Collaboration

Two important relationships may occur between human skills and agentic skills when a skill configuration is constructed: **skills competition** and **skills collaboration**.

**Skills competition** occurs when both a human skill and an agentic skill can satisfy the same or similar requirements for an action, but both do not need to be applied simultaneously. Under a particular occupational and task context, one may be better suited than the other.

For example, both a strategic business analyst and an AI agent may be capable of comparing two documented business processes and identifying differences. When the process definitions are complete, structured, and based on explicit rules, the agentic skill may provide faster and more systematic comparison. When the process documentation is incomplete and important differences depend on informal organizational practices, the analyst's human understanding may provide the better fit.

The purpose of skills competition is therefore not to establish whether human or agentic skills are generally superior. It is to identify which available skill provides the better fit for a particular action under particular conditions.

**Skills collaboration** occurs when the requirements of a task or its actions cannot be satisfied effectively by one skill type alone, or when combining complementary human and agentic skills is expected to produce better task performance.

Collaboration does not require human and agentic skills to operate at exactly the same time. They may be activated at different stages of action execution in a **sequential, iterative, or recursive manner**.

For example, an analyst may facilitate stakeholder discussions to define a business problem, an AI agent may organize the resulting information and identify unresolved requirements, the analyst may return to stakeholders to resolve those gaps, the agent may compare alternative future-state options, and the analyst may use the evidence to refine a recommendation. Each cycle can produce new information that changes the next set of actions.

This dynamic relationship allows strategic business analysts to exploit the complementary strengths of both skill types rather than relying on a permanent allocation of work.

### 1.3.2. Skill Configuration and Task Execution

A skill configuration specifies which human skills and agentic skills will be used to execute the actions associated with a task or subtask and how those skills will be coordinated.

The configuration depends on factors such as strategic importance, task ambiguity, stakeholder diversity, information quality, analytical complexity, process maturity, available tools, expected reliability, time constraints, organizational risk, and intermediate results.

For one action, an agentic skill may independently satisfy the requirements. For another, a human skill may be selected instead. A complex strategic initiative may require several configurations across multiple actions, with human and agentic skills repeatedly interacting as the analysis progresses.

The configuration is therefore dynamic rather than permanent. It may change when stakeholder information changes, new data becomes available, an assumption proves invalid, an agentic result fails validation, or human interpretation identifies a new problem or opportunity.

Throughout this process, the strategic business analyst remains the professional responsible for promoting and controlling the work associated with the occupation and its responsibilities. Agentic AI provides powerful complementary capabilities that can expand what the professional can retrieve, analyze, compare, model, document, validate, and communicate.

### 1.3.3. Task Performance Evaluation and Skill Space Adaptation

The performance produced through a selected skill configuration must be evaluated against **predefined evaluation metrics**. These metrics determine whether the human skills, agentic skills, or their integrated configuration are sufficiently capable of performing the relevant task and its required actions.

The evaluation criteria depend on the work being performed. In strategic business analysis, they may include analytical accuracy, completeness, consistency, requirement quality, stakeholder acceptance, traceability, business relevance, strategic alignment, clarity, timeliness, feasibility, and reliability.

When a selected skill or skill configuration produces performance above the defined threshold, it passes the performance evaluation and can be retained for future applications under appropriate conditions. Repeated successful use provides additional evidence about where that skill or configuration can be applied effectively.

When performance falls below the required threshold, the corresponding skill or configuration should not simply be reused without modification. Human or agentic skills may be refined or reconfigured. Existing skills may also be combined differently, or new human and agentic skills may be developed or constructed when the existing skill space does not provide an adequate capability.

For example, if an agentic skill produces well-structured requirements but repeatedly misses dependencies between organizational units, it may be refined through better context, additional source information, dependency-analysis procedures, or a human-review stage. If the analyst lacks the knowledge required to evaluate a new business capability or technology domain, the corresponding human skill may be developed through research, learning, and practical experience.

Refined and newly constructed skills that satisfy the required performance criteria can be added to the skill space. Task performance evaluation therefore provides feedback not only for completing the current work but also for continuously optimizing and expanding available capabilities.

The skill space should not be constructed as an isolated collection of occupation-specific representations. Many capabilities used in strategic business analysis are reusable across occupations. Information retrieval, data analysis, process modeling, financial reasoning, requirements analysis, comparison, summarization, communication, visualization, facilitation, and tool use may also contribute to management consulting, product management, project management, operations analysis, systems analysis, financial analysis, and other professional work.

Reusable skills can therefore be represented once and configured differently according to the responsibilities, tasks, actions, and contexts of different occupations. Occupational specialization can be introduced through business context, task requirements, parameters, stakeholder information, tools, constraints, and skill configurations rather than unnecessarily creating a separate representation whenever the same underlying capability appears elsewhere.

The resulting process is adaptive:

**occupational responsibility → task and subtask → actions → skill requirements → candidate human and agentic skills → skill configuration → task execution → task performance evaluation → skill refinement, reconfiguration, or construction → optimized and expanded skill space**

This cycle allows human and agentic capabilities to evolve together with the requirements of strategic business analysis.

## 1.4. Essential Responsibilities of Strategic Business Analyst

### 1.4.1. Strategic Needs, Opportunities, and Current-State Analysis

Strategic needs, opportunities, and current-state analysis establishes what business problem or opportunity should be addressed, why change may be required, and how the present organization, capabilities, processes, information, and constraints affect possible responses.

The responsibility may involve analyzing organizational goals, examining current performance, identifying capability gaps, conducting research, studying internal and external influences, defining desired outcomes, and identifying risks or constraints that may affect future decisions.

Human skills are especially important for interpreting organizational objectives, recognizing implicit assumptions, understanding political and operational realities, and distinguishing symptoms from underlying business problems. Agentic skills can complement this work through information retrieval, document analysis, research synthesis, data comparison, trend identification, and structured current-state assessment.

For example, senior stakeholders may report that a business function is responding too slowly to customer requests. The analyst first explores what "too slowly" means in relation to strategic objectives and stakeholder expectations. An AI agent can analyze performance reports, process documentation, historical issues, and available operational data to identify patterns. The analyst reviews those findings and recognizes that the apparent response-time problem may originate in an upstream approval process rather than the customer-facing activity itself. The agent is then asked to investigate that dependency, allowing the problem definition to be refined iteratively.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|strategic reasoning, systems thinking, business-context interpretation, problem framing, professional judgment|information retrieval, research synthesis, document analysis, trend identification, evidence organization|The analyst frames and refines the strategic problem while the AI agent expands the evidence that can be examined and compares current-state conditions across multiple sources.|

### 1.4.2. Stakeholder Engagement and Requirements Analysis

Stakeholder engagement and requirements analysis identifies the needs, expectations, constraints, and desired outcomes of the people and groups affected by a business initiative and translates them into clear and usable requirements.

The responsibility includes stakeholder identification, interviews, workshops, requirements elicitation, validation, prioritization, conflict resolution, requirement documentation, and maintenance of alignment between business needs and proposed solutions.

Agentic skills can support meeting preparation, document extraction, requirement drafting, requirement classification, inconsistency detection, traceability, summarization, and comparison across stakeholder inputs. Human skills remain central to facilitation, relationship building, detecting ambiguity, interpreting organizational interests, resolving disagreement, and determining which requirements truly support strategic outcomes.

For example, several business units may provide conflicting requirements for a proposed change. An AI agent can organize the statements, identify areas of agreement and contradiction, and generate questions requiring clarification. The analyst facilitates a workshop in which stakeholders explain their underlying needs. The agent then updates the consolidated requirements and identifies remaining gaps. The analyst uses this evidence to negotiate a feasible set of requirements that supports the broader business objective.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|stakeholder facilitation, requirements elicitation, negotiation, contextual interpretation, communication|requirement extraction, classification, comparison, consistency checking, traceability support|The AI agent structures and checks stakeholder information while the analyst elicits underlying needs, resolves conflicts, and ensures that requirements remain aligned with strategic objectives.|

### 1.4.3. Business Process Analysis, Architecture, and Improvement

Business process analysis, architecture, and improvement examines how work is currently performed, identifies inefficiencies and dependencies, and develops improved future-state processes that better support organizational objectives.

The responsibility may include mapping current processes, identifying handoffs and bottlenecks, analyzing business capabilities, comparing current and future states, performing gap analysis, identifying opportunities for automation, and designing improved workflows.

Agentic skills can support process-document extraction, process-map generation, comparison of current and future states, identification of repeated steps, dependency analysis, and generation of alternative workflows. Human skills are especially important for understanding why processes developed in their current form, identifying informal practices, assessing organizational feasibility, and determining whether a proposed improvement is likely to create real value.

For example, a process may contain several approval stages that appear redundant. An AI agent can map the workflow, calculate cycle times, and identify duplicated information exchanges. The analyst interviews participants and discovers that one apparently redundant approval exists because of a risk condition not captured in the formal documentation. The analyst and agent then explore alternatives that preserve the required control while reducing unnecessary delay.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|process analysis, systems thinking, organizational understanding, improvement judgment, facilitation|process mapping, gap analysis, workflow comparison, dependency analysis, improvement-option generation|The AI agent makes complex process relationships easier to inspect while the analyst determines why those relationships exist and which future-state changes are operationally appropriate.|

### 1.4.4. Business Data, Performance, and Decision-Support Analysis

Business data, performance, and decision-support analysis uses quantitative and qualitative information to understand business performance, identify patterns, evaluate outcomes, and support strategic and operational decisions.

The responsibility may involve analyzing operational data, defining performance indicators, developing dashboards, validating information quality, identifying trends, conducting root-cause analysis, and translating analytical results into business insights.

Agentic skills can contribute extensively through data extraction, calculation, validation, trend analysis, anomaly detection, visualization generation, repeated comparison, and automated reporting. Human skills remain important for selecting meaningful measures, interpreting analytical results, distinguishing correlation from explanation, and connecting quantitative findings with organizational context.

For example, an AI agent may identify a decline in a key performance measure and show that it coincides with changes in several operational variables. The analyst determines that the observed relationships are insufficient to identify the cause and asks the agent to segment the information by customer type, process stage, and time period. The additional analysis reveals a concentrated performance problem. The analyst then relates the pattern to a recent process change and recommends further investigation.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|analytical reasoning, performance-measure design, business interpretation, root-cause reasoning, decision judgment|data extraction, calculation, validation, trend analysis, visualization, anomaly detection|The AI agent expands the scale and speed of business analysis while the analyst determines which measures matter, interprets patterns, and converts analytical evidence into decision-relevant insights.|

### 1.4.5. Business Case Development and Solution Evaluation

Business case development and solution evaluation assesses alternative responses to a business need and determines whether a proposed solution is expected to create sufficient value relative to its costs, risks, constraints, and resource requirements.

The responsibility may include researching solution options, assessing capabilities, comparing technologies or operating approaches, estimating benefits and costs, conducting financial analysis, evaluating risks, modeling scenarios, defining expected outcomes, and recommending preferred alternatives.

Agentic skills can support market and technical research, option comparison, financial calculations, scenario generation, risk organization, assumption testing, and preparation of business-case drafts. Human skills remain essential for determining whether alternatives genuinely address the business need, evaluating strategic alignment, interpreting nonfinancial consequences, challenging assumptions, and making defensible recommendations.

For example, an organization may be considering several ways to improve a business capability. An AI agent can gather available information, compare solution characteristics, estimate costs under different assumptions, and summarize expected benefits and risks. The analyst determines that the least expensive option would require substantial process changes that could reduce expected adoption. The agent is asked to model an alternative scenario incorporating transition costs and implementation risk. The analyst then uses the expanded evidence to recommend the option most likely to deliver sustainable business value.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|business-case reasoning, strategic alignment, financial judgment, risk evaluation, recommendation development|research, option comparison, financial calculation, scenario modeling, assumption testing, business-case drafting|The AI agent broadens and accelerates analysis of solution alternatives while the analyst evaluates strategic value, feasibility, risks, and organizational consequences before making a recommendation.|

### 1.4.6. Solution Validation, Implementation Readiness, and Change Support

Solution validation, implementation readiness, and change support helps ensure that a selected solution satisfies business requirements, can be adopted by the organization, and produces the intended business outcomes.

The responsibility may include validating solution designs, defining acceptance criteria, supporting testing, identifying implementation gaps, analyzing change impacts, preparing transition requirements, supporting training and documentation, and evaluating whether the organization is ready for the future state.

Agentic skills can support requirement-to-solution comparison, test-case generation, validation checks, change-impact analysis, issue classification, documentation generation, and identification of unresolved requirements. Human skills are particularly important for determining whether the solution works in real business conditions, evaluating stakeholder readiness, coordinating corrective decisions, and resolving issues that span organizational and technical boundaries.

For example, a proposed solution may pass its initial functional tests while users report that an important business scenario remains difficult to complete. An AI agent can compare the issue with documented requirements, identify affected process steps, and generate candidate test cases. The analyst works with stakeholders to determine whether the problem reflects a missing requirement, a design limitation, or a training issue. The agent then supports another validation cycle based on the revised understanding.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|solution validation, change-impact interpretation, stakeholder coordination, readiness assessment, professional judgment|requirement comparison, test generation, issue classification, impact analysis, documentation support|The AI agent systematically checks solutions against documented expectations while the analyst evaluates whether the solution is usable, adoptable, and capable of achieving the intended business outcome.|

### 1.4.7. Executive Communication, Documentation, and Strategic Advisory Support

Executive communication, documentation, and strategic advisory support converts complex analytical work into clear information that decision-makers and other stakeholders can understand and use.

The responsibility includes preparing business-analysis artifacts, dashboards, reports, presentations, recommendations, decision materials, process models, business cases, and other documentation. It also involves explaining findings, facilitating decisions, maintaining shared understanding, and supporting knowledge transfer across business and technical groups.

Agentic skills can support drafting, summarization, visualization preparation, document organization, comparison of source information, presentation development, and adaptation of technical material for different audiences. Human skills remain central to selecting the most decision-relevant information, understanding stakeholder concerns, presenting recommendations persuasively, responding to questions, and exercising judgment about what should be emphasized.

For example, an AI agent may prepare a draft executive briefing summarizing a process-improvement analysis, proposed solution options, expected benefits, risks, and unresolved issues. The analyst reviews the briefing and determines that one risk requires greater emphasis because it could affect the strategic objective. During the leadership discussion, new questions arise. The analyst uses agentic analysis to investigate the requested scenarios and then provides an updated recommendation.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|executive communication, strategic advisory judgment, stakeholder understanding, presentation, recommendation explanation|report drafting, summarization, visualization preparation, document organization, presentation support|The AI agent accelerates preparation and synthesis of complex analytical material while the analyst determines what matters for the decision and communicates its strategic significance to stakeholders.|

## 1.5. Changing Human and Agentic Involvement Across Strategic Business Analysis Responsibilities

Human skills and agentic skills are involved throughout strategic business analysis, but their relative involvement differs across responsibilities and may change during task execution.

|Responsibility|Typical human involvement|Typical agentic involvement|Typical integration|
|---|---|---|---|
|Strategic needs, opportunities, and current-state analysis|Very high|Very high|Agentic research and evidence synthesis combined with human strategic interpretation and problem framing.|
|Stakeholder engagement and requirements analysis|Very high|High|Agentic requirement organization and validation combined with human elicitation, facilitation, and conflict resolution.|
|Business process analysis, architecture, and improvement|Very high|Very high|Agentic process mapping and comparison combined with human organizational interpretation and redesign judgment.|
|Business data, performance, and decision-support analysis|High|Very high|Agentic data analysis and visualization combined with human interpretation and decision reasoning.|
|Business case development and solution evaluation|Very high|Very high|Agentic research, financial analysis, and scenario modeling combined with human strategic, risk, and value judgment.|
|Solution validation, implementation readiness, and change support|Very high|Very high|Agentic validation and impact analysis combined with human readiness assessment and stakeholder coordination.|
|Executive communication, documentation, and strategic advisory support|Very high|High|Agentic synthesis and document preparation combined with human advisory judgment, communication, and decision facilitation.|

These involvement levels should not be interpreted as permanent allocations. A single responsibility may contain actions in which human and agentic skills compete as alternative capabilities and other actions in which they collaborate sequentially, iteratively, or recursively.

The practical objective is to construct skill configurations that use the most suitable available capabilities for each part of the work and to revise those configurations when task performance evaluation shows that another arrangement could perform more effectively or reliably.

## 1.6. Working as a Strategic Business Analyst with Agentic AI

Agentic AI is becoming an increasingly powerful and promising part of strategic business analysis. Strategic business analysts should therefore be prepared to accept and embrace AI agents as complementary participants in professional workflows rather than treating them only as occasional tools for research, document preparation, or data analysis.

This does not reduce the importance of strategic business analysis expertise. Human professionals remain responsible for the occupation and its corresponding responsibilities. They understand organizational objectives, interpret stakeholder interests, frame business problems, evaluate strategic alternatives, facilitate difficult decisions, understand organizational change, control consequential recommendations, and determine whether task performance is acceptable.

At the same time, agentic skills can substantially expand the amount and variety of information that can be processed. AI agents can search large bodies of information, analyze business data, compare stakeholder requirements, generate process models, identify inconsistencies, evaluate scenarios, prepare business-case materials, support solution validation, and continuously organize evidence at a scale difficult to achieve through direct human effort alone.

The strongest strategic business analysis practice is therefore not based on maximizing human work or maximizing agentic work independently. It is based on finding appropriate configurations of human skills and agentic skills for the responsibilities, tasks, subtasks, and actions being performed.

Where a human skill and an agentic skill can independently perform the same action, skills competition can help identify which capability is better suited to the current context. Where complex business work requires complementary capabilities, skills collaboration can activate human and agentic skills sequentially, iteratively, or recursively to achieve stronger task performance.

Task performance evaluation then determines whether the selected skills and configurations satisfy predefined expectations for analytical quality, strategic alignment, completeness, stakeholder usefulness, feasibility, reliability, and other relevant requirements. Successful capabilities can be retained and reused, while inadequate capabilities can be refined, reconfigured, combined differently, or newly developed. Reusable capabilities can subsequently contribute not only to strategic business analysis but also to other occupations requiring related analytical, communication, process, financial, and decision-support skills.

For a strategic business analyst, working effectively with agentic AI therefore means treating AI as a powerful complementary capability that can be integrated into everyday professional routines while maintaining human control of occupational responsibilities. Through appropriate skill selection, competition, collaboration, evaluation, and continuous improvement, strategic business analysts and agentic AI can work together to produce business analysis that is more comprehensive, efficient, evidence-based, adaptive, and strategically valuable.
