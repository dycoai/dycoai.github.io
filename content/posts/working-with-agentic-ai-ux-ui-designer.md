# 1. Working with Agentic AI: UX/UI Designer

*How human skills and agentic skills are dynamically integrated and coordinated across UX/UI design responsibilities*

## 1.1. UX/UI Designer as an Occupation

A **UX/UI designer** designs and optimizes digital interfaces and user experiences by conducting user research, creating wireframes and prototypes, designing visual elements, and conducting usability testing. This occupation connects user needs with technical implementation by defining interaction models, information architectures, visual languages, and usability standards that support intuitiveness, accessibility, engagement, and operational effectiveness. To ensure design decisions support long-term user and organizational objectives, the designer must understand how user psychology, business goals, technical constraints, accessibility standards, platform guidelines, and behavioral data collectively shape the digital product landscape.

Typical responsibilities of a UX/UI designer include:

* user research and persona development;
* information architecture and user flow design;
* wireframing and interactive prototyping;
* visual design and design system management;
* usability testing and heuristic evaluation;
* accessibility and inclusive design compliance;
* developer handoff and implementation quality assurance; and
* product strategy and experience metrics analysis.

These responsibilities are fulfilled through distinct tasks and subtasks, each requiring one or more actions with specific skill requirements. Human skills and agentic skills both contribute to these responsibilities, but their levels and forms of involvement differ according to the responsibility, task, action, design scope, organizational context, system complexity, technical environment, and expected outcome.

## 1.2. Human Skills and Agentic Skills in UX/UI Design

UX/UI design increasingly integrates both **human skills** and **agentic skills**.

Human skills are reusable capabilities possessed and developed by UX/UI designers. They include user empathy, user research, interaction design, visual reasoning, systems thinking, accessibility reasoning, strategic alignment, contextual interpretation, facilitation, negotiation, and professional judgment.

Agentic skills are reusable capabilities available to AI agents through their underlying models, contextual information, instructions, constraints, tools, and computational resources. In UX/UI design, these skills include information retrieval, behavioral data analysis, heatmap generation, wireframe generation, component library scanning, contrast checking, code extraction, usability metric aggregation, scenario simulation, and tool use.

Both skill types contribute to the same occupational responsibilities, but their involvement varies by action. Defining the strategic direction of a digital product relies primarily on human understanding of user psychology and business objectives; an AI agent then uses agentic skills to retrieve, organize, and compare relevant behavioral data and market benchmarks. In interaction design, the designer first establishes the core user flows, and an AI agent subsequently generates wireframe variations and maps edge-case states. For design system maintenance, an AI agent scans existing component libraries for visual inconsistencies, while the designer interprets the deviations, evaluates brand alignment, and determines the appropriate structural response. Across these actions, human and agentic skills are activated sequentially or iteratively based on task requirements, with the human professional maintaining oversight and final judgment.

The relationship between these skills is not a fixed division where certain responsibilities permanently belong to humans and others to AI. Instead, human and agentic involvement shifts across tasks and between different actions within the same task.

## 1.3. Dynamic Human–Agentic Skill Integration and Collaboration in UX/UI Design

The work of a UX/UI designer can be understood as a structured set of responsibilities fulfilled through tasks and subtasks, which are executed through actions requiring appropriate configurations of human and agentic skills.

An **occupation** provides the overall professional context. For a UX/UI designer, this context encompasses understanding user needs, defining interaction models and information architectures, designing visual interfaces, governing usability standards, and maintaining coherence across digital product experiences.

A **responsibility** represents an expected area of professional work or an outcome that the UX/UI designer accomplishes or maintains. Responsibilities such as user research, wireframing, design system management, and usability testing each contain multiple tasks.

A **task** is a **context-dependent unit of work** used to fulfill part or all of a responsibility. When a task is too broad or complex for reliable execution, it is decomposed into smaller **subtasks**.

Tasks and subtasks are executed through one or more **actions**. Actions represent **reusable and configurable operations** such as analyzing behavioral telemetry, mapping user flows, generating wireframe alternatives, evaluating component consistency, checking accessibility contrast, or synthesizing usability feedback.

Each action has **skill requirements**. These requirements describe the capabilities needed for effective and reliable execution. Depending on the action and its context, human skills, agentic skills, or an integration of both can satisfy these requirements.

The skills available for UX/UI design reside in a **skill space** containing both human skills and agentic skills. Relevant skills in this space can serve as candidate skills for executing the actions required by a particular task. A human skill in user empathy, for example, can serve as a candidate for interpreting the emotional impact of a friction point, while an agentic skill in heatmap generation can serve as a candidate for identifying where that friction occurs across a user interface.

Candidate skills are evaluated relative to the requirements of the action and its occupational and task context. Appropriate skills are then selected and organized into a **skill configuration** for execution. Depending on these requirements, a configuration may rely mainly on human skills, mainly on agentic skills, or on an integrated arrangement of both.

### 1.3.1. Skills Competition and Skills Collaboration

Two important relationships can occur between human skills and agentic skills when constructing a skill configuration: **skills competition** and **skills collaboration**.

**Skills competition** occurs when both a human skill and an agentic skill can satisfy the same or similar requirements for an action, but simultaneous application of both is unnecessary. Under a particular occupational and task context, one skill type can provide a better fit for the action than the other.

For example, both a UX/UI designer and an AI agent can evaluate a proposed interface against standard heuristic principles. When the heuristics are explicit, the design artifacts are sufficiently complete, and conformance can be evaluated through defined visual criteria, the agentic skill provides fast and consistent execution. When the assessment involves complex user mental models, ambiguous business constraints, or a justified exception to an established design pattern, the designer's human skills provide the contextual reasoning required for the action.

The purpose of skills competition is not to establish whether human or agentic skills are generally superior. Rather, it identifies which available skill provides the better fit for a particular action under particular conditions.

**Skills collaboration** occurs when one skill type alone cannot effectively satisfy the requirements of a task or its actions, or when combining complementary human and agentic skills produces stronger task performance.

Collaboration does not require human and agentic skills to operate simultaneously. Instead, they are activated at different stages of action execution in a **sequential, iterative, or recursive manner** according to evolving task requirements.

For example, a UX/UI designer first identifies that users are abandoning a critical onboarding sequence and determines which interaction variables require investigation. An AI agent then examines session recordings, heatmap data, and user feedback transcripts, identifying potential areas of friction and patterns that may indicate cognitive difficulty. The designer subsequently interprets these findings in relation to user mental models and develops several interaction alternatives. Based on the designer's direction, the agent next compares the alternatives across defined criteria such as estimated task completion time, accessibility compliance, and development complexity. Finally, the designer evaluates the resulting evidence and determines which interaction direction should guide subsequent prototyping. Throughout this sequence, human and agentic skills are activated iteratively as task requirements evolve, while the designer directs the analysis and evaluates its outcomes.

This dynamic relationship allows UX/UI designers to leverage the complementary strengths of both skill types rather than relying on a permanent allocation of work.

### 1.3.2. Skill Configuration and Task Execution

A **skill configuration** specifies which human skills and agentic skills are used to execute the actions associated with a task or subtask and how those skills are coordinated.

The configuration depends on factors such as action requirements, design scope, platform complexity, user ambiguity, available behavioral data, technical dependencies, expected reliability, accessibility constraints, organizational impact, decision consequences, stakeholder expectations, available tools, and intermediate results.

For one action, an agentic skill may independently satisfy the requirements. For another, a human skill may provide the better fit. A complex UX/UI design task may require several configurations across multiple actions, with human and agentic skills repeatedly interacting as the task progresses.

The configuration is therefore dynamic rather than permanent. It may change when the action changes, new user research becomes available, organizational priorities shift, an agentic result fails to satisfy expected requirements, or human interpretation identifies a new interaction direction.

Throughout this process, the UX/UI designer remains the professional responsible for directing and controlling the work associated with the occupation and its responsibilities. Agentic AI provides powerful complementary capabilities that expand what the professional can retrieve, map, generate, analyze, prototype, evaluate, and communicate.

### 1.3.3. Task Performance Evaluation and Skill Space Adaptation

The performance produced through a selected skill configuration is evaluated against **predefined evaluation metrics**. These metrics determine whether the human skills, agentic skills, or their integrated configuration are sufficiently capable of performing the relevant task and its required actions.

Evaluation criteria depend on the work being performed. In UX/UI design, they include user satisfaction, task success rate, interaction consistency, accessibility compliance, visual alignment, cognitive load reduction, development feasibility, interpretability, decision usefulness, and other task-specific expectations.

When a selected skill or skill configuration produces performance above the defined threshold, it passes the performance evaluation and is retained for future applications under appropriate conditions. Repeated successful use provides additional evidence about where that skill or configuration performs effectively.

When performance falls below the required threshold, the corresponding skill or configuration is not reused without modification. The relevant human or agentic skills may be refined or reconfigured, existing skills can be combined differently, or new human and agentic skills may be developed or constructed from scratch when the existing skill space lacks adequate capability.

For example, an AI agent first generates accessibility reports for a new mobile interface, but task performance evaluation shows that it repeatedly misses context-dependent touch-target violations caused by dynamic overlays. The agentic skill is then refined through improved contextual instructions, additional interface state information, stronger spatial-analysis procedures, or validation against approved interaction models. The revised skill is evaluated again, and once it satisfies the required performance criteria, it is retained for appropriate future compliance tasks. Human skills follow a corresponding development process: when a designer lacks sufficient knowledge of an emerging spatial computing paradigm, the relevant capability is developed through learning, collaboration, and practical experience before its performance is evaluated through subsequent work.

Refined and newly constructed skills that satisfy the required performance criteria are added to the skill space. In this way, task performance evaluation provides feedback not only for completing current work but also for continuously optimizing and expanding the available skill space.

The skill space is not constructed as an isolated collection of occupation-specific representations. Many human and agentic skills used in UX/UI design are reusable across occupations. User empathy, systems thinking, information retrieval, behavioral analysis, scenario simulation, code extraction, visual reasoning, communication, and tool use contribute to work in product management, software engineering, marketing, customer success, business analysis, and many other fields.

Reusable skills can therefore be represented once and configured differently according to the responsibilities, tasks, actions, and contexts of different occupations. This allows the skill space to serve a growing range of occupations without unnecessarily duplicating or exploding the number of skill representations. Occupational specialization is introduced through task context, action requirements, design domains, parameters, information sources, tools, constraints, and skill configurations rather than by creating a completely separate skill representation whenever the same underlying capability appears in another occupation.

The resulting process is adaptive:

**occupational responsibility → task and subtask → actions → skill requirements → candidate human and agentic skills → skill configuration → task execution → task performance evaluation → skill refinement, reconfiguration, or construction → optimized and expanded skill space**

This cycle allows human and agentic capabilities to evolve alongside the requirements of UX/UI design work.

## 1.4. Essential Responsibilities of UX/UI Designer

### 1.4.1. User Research and Persona Development

User research and persona development establishes the foundational understanding of user needs, behaviors, and motivations required to guide product strategy. This responsibility includes defining research objectives, analyzing behavioral data, conducting user interviews, synthesizing qualitative feedback, and developing representative personas and journey maps.

Agentic skills contribute to behavioral data analysis, support ticket summarization, survey response clustering, sentiment analysis, and persona template generation. Human skills remain essential for cultivating user empathy, interpreting unstated emotional drivers, resolving conflicting user needs, and defining personas that accurately reflect the target audience within business constraints.

For example, a UX/UI designer first defines the strategic objectives that the future product experience must support and identifies the major user segments requiring investigation. An AI agent then examines available behavioral analytics, support tickets, survey responses, and research materials, summarizing recurring user behaviors, needs, and pain points. The designer combines this evidence with findings from user interviews and develops evidence-based persona profiles and journey maps. Based on the designer's criteria, the agent next compares the identified user segments across prevalence, behavioral patterns, needs, and relevance to the product's objectives. Finally, the designer resolves the major trade-offs and establishes the primary user personas that guide subsequent design work.

| Human skills                                                                                                   | Agentic skills                                                                                                       | Integrated collaboration                                                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| user empathy, strategic alignment, qualitative synthesis, emotional reasoning, persona formulation | behavioral data analysis, survey clustering, sentiment analysis, template generation, comparative analysis | The designer defines strategic research direction and formulates user empathy, while the AI agent organizes behavioral evidence, clusters feedback, and compares persona alternatives. |

### 1.4.2. Information Architecture and User Flow Design

Information architecture and user flow design translates user needs and business requirements into structured navigation models, content hierarchies, and interaction pathways. This responsibility includes auditing existing structures, defining mental models, mapping user flows, identifying navigation bottlenecks, and establishing architectural views that connect content with user intent.

Agentic skills are well suited to extracting user navigation behaviors from analytics platforms, mapping navigation paths, identifying dead ends, comparing structural alternatives, and generating flow diagrams. Human skills contribute to determining the intuitive logic of the interface, resolving ambiguous content relationships, defining future-state mental models, and deciding how structural changes should influence user cognition.

For example, an AI agent first extracts the current site map and user navigation paths from analytics platforms, preparing a preliminary structural audit. The UX/UI designer reviews the structure and identifies areas where legacy business logic distorts the intuitive user journey. The designer then restructures the information architecture around the mental models required by the target user base. Based on the revised structure, the agent maps supporting content dependencies and generates updated user flow diagrams. Finally, the designer validates the resulting information architecture and determines which navigation bottlenecks require immediate redesign.

| Human skills                                                                                                                    | Agentic skills                                                                                                  | Integrated collaboration                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| mental modeling, cognitive reasoning, structural prioritization, navigation strategy, content interpretation | site map extraction, path mapping, dead-end identification, flow diagram generation, structural documentation | The AI agent accelerates extraction and mapping of navigation data, while the designer defines the cognitive structure and determines its strategic significance for the user. |

### 1.4.3. Wireframing and Interactive Prototyping

Wireframing and interactive prototyping translates conceptual architectures into tangible, testable interface layouts and interaction sequences. This responsibility includes defining interaction paradigms, generating layout variations, mapping state changes, linking screens, and establishing prototypes that simulate the final user experience.

Agentic skills efficiently generate low-fidelity wireframe variations, map edge-case states, link interactive screens, and simulate responsive breakpoints. Human skills remain essential for defining the core interaction logic, evaluating cognitive load, determining appropriate visual weight, and establishing interaction patterns that guide user attention.

For example, the UX/UI designer first defines the core interaction paradigms and success metrics for a new checkout feature. An AI agent then generates several low-fidelity wireframe variations and maps the required state changes for each interaction path. The designer reviews the alternatives and identifies where certain layouts create unacceptable cognitive load. Based on the designer's revised constraints, the agent generates an interactive prototype and links the approved screens. Finally, the designer tests the interactive prototype and establishes the corresponding interaction guidance for the development team.

| Human skills                                                                                                                   | Agentic skills                                                                                               | Integrated collaboration                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| interaction logic, cognitive load evaluation, visual weight distribution, state definition, prototyping strategy | wireframe generation, state mapping, screen linking, breakpoint simulation, layout variation | The AI agent systematically generates layout variations and maps interaction states, while the designer defines the core interaction logic and governs the cognitive flow of the prototype. |

### 1.4.4. Visual Design and Design System Management

Visual design and design system management establishes and maintains the visual language, component libraries, and design tokens that ensure consistency, brand alignment, and scalability across digital products. This responsibility includes defining visual direction, auditing component libraries, updating design tokens, resolving visual inconsistencies, and governing design system evolution.

Agentic skills efficiently scan component libraries for visual deviations, extract design tokens, identify inconsistent spacing or typography, generate updated component documentation, and compare designs against brand guidelines. Human skills remain essential for interpreting brand strategy, defining the aesthetic direction, resolving edge-case component behaviors, and establishing visual hierarchies that support usability.

For example, the UX/UI designer first establishes the visual direction, typography scales, and brand alignment for a major product refresh. An AI agent then scans the existing component library, identifies visual inconsistencies, and summarizes the required updates to match the new design tokens. The designer reviews the flagged components and determines which deviations require structural redesign versus simple token updates. Based on the designer's decisions, the agent updates the design system files and generates the revised component documentation. Finally, the designer assesses the updated design system and determines whether it satisfies the organization's brand and scalability criteria.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| brand interpretation, aesthetic strategy, visual hierarchy definition, component governance, edge-case resolution | library scanning, token extraction, deviation identification, documentation generation, guideline comparison | The AI agent systematically scans component libraries and identifies visual deviations, while the designer defines the aesthetic strategy and governs the structural evolution of the design system. |

### 1.4.5. Usability Testing and Heuristic Evaluation

Usability testing and heuristic evaluation validates design decisions against actual user behavior and established usability principles, identifying friction points and areas for iterative improvement. This responsibility includes defining test objectives, designing test scenarios, analyzing session telemetry, synthesizing qualitative feedback, and prioritizing design iterations.

Agentic skills support session recording analysis, heatmap generation, task completion metric aggregation, heuristic checklist scanning, and qualitative feedback summarization. Human skills remain essential for designing targeted test scenarios, interpreting the root causes of user frustration, evaluating heuristic exceptions, and determining the appropriate design iteration.

For example, a UX/UI designer first establishes the usability testing objectives and defines the critical user tasks required to evaluate a new dashboard. An AI agent then analyzes existing session recordings and heatmap data, identifying potential friction points to include in the test scenarios. The designer reviews the findings and designs targeted usability tasks that probe these specific cognitive bottlenecks. Based on the designer's test plan, the agent aggregates the subsequent testing metrics and summarizes qualitative user feedback. Finally, the designer interprets the synthesized evidence and determines which interaction patterns require immediate iteration.

| Human skills                                                                                                       | Agentic skills                                                                                      | Integrated collaboration                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| test scenario design, cognitive interpretation, friction root-cause analysis, iteration prioritization, heuristic judgment | session analysis, heatmap generation, metric aggregation, checklist scanning, feedback summarization | The AI agent performs systematic telemetry analysis and organizes testing evidence, while the designer designs the test scenarios and determines the appropriate iterative design response. |

### 1.4.6. Accessibility and Inclusive Design Compliance

Accessibility and inclusive design compliance ensures that digital interfaces are usable by individuals with diverse abilities, adhering to established standards such as WCAG and organizational inclusion mandates. This responsibility includes defining accessibility requirements, scanning designs for violations, evaluating alternative interaction patterns, and ensuring that design decisions support equitable access.

Agentic skills support contrast checking, touch-target measurement, screen-reader compatibility scanning, semantic HTML validation, and compliance report generation. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the real-world impact of exclusion, designing inclusive alternative interactions, resolving conflicts between aesthetic goals and accessibility rules, and determining the appropriate remediation strategy.

For example, the UX/UI designer first establishes the inclusive design requirements and accessibility standards relevant to a new mobile application. An AI agent then compares the proposed interface designs with established WCAG guidelines, checking color contrast, touch-target sizes, and screen-reader compatibility, and identifies potential violations. The designer reviews the findings and determines which issues represent material exclusion and which can be addressed through alternative interaction patterns. Based on the designer's decisions, the agent updates the compliance report and evaluates the revised designs against the relevant standards. Finally, the designer assesses the residual risk and determines whether the interface satisfies the organization's inclusive design criteria.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| inclusive strategy, exclusion impact interpretation, alternative interaction design, compliance judgment, aesthetic remediation | contrast checking, target measurement, compatibility scanning, semantic validation, report generation | The AI agent performs systematic guideline comparison and identifies accessibility violations, while the designer interprets the impact of exclusion and designs the appropriate inclusive interaction patterns. |

### 1.4.7. Developer Handoff and Implementation Quality Assurance

Developer handoff and implementation quality assurance connects design specifications with engineering execution, ensuring that the final coded product accurately reflects the intended user experience and interaction logic. This responsibility includes documenting interaction states, generating design tokens, identifying front-end constraints, reviewing staging environments, and negotiating technical trade-offs.

Agentic skills support CSS extraction, design token generation, state documentation, staging environment scanning, and implementation discrepancy identification. Human skills remain essential for defining handoff standards, negotiating feasible technical compromises with engineers, protecting the core user experience during implementation, and determining the final quality assurance approval.

For example, business and engineering leaders first request that a complex data visualization feature be handed off for development. The UX/UI designer defines which interaction states, responsive behaviors, and edge cases must be documented. An AI agent then extracts the design specifications, generates the corresponding CSS and component properties, and identifies potential implementation conflicts with the existing front-end framework. The designer reviews the generated specifications, discusses the technical trade-offs with the engineering team, and determines which design adjustments are feasible without undermining the user experience. Based on the agreed direction, the agent updates the handoff documentation and supporting design tokens. Finally, the designer communicates the finalized specifications and ensures that the implementation quality remains aligned with the original design intent.

| Human skills                                                                                             | Agentic skills                                                                                    | Integrated collaboration                                                                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| handoff standard definition, technical negotiation, experience protection, trade-off reasoning, QA approval | CSS extraction, token generation, state documentation, staging scanning, discrepancy identification | The AI agent extracts specifications and identifies implementation conflicts, while the designer negotiates technical trade-offs and protects the structural integrity of the intended user experience. |

### 1.4.8. Product Strategy and Experience Metrics Analysis

Product strategy and experience metrics analysis connects design outcomes with organizational business objectives, ensuring that user experience improvements drive measurable value. This responsibility includes defining experience KPIs, aggregating behavioral metrics, analyzing conversion funnels, interpreting sentiment shifts, and aligning design roadmaps with product strategy.

Agentic skills support metric aggregation, funnel analysis, baseline comparison, sentiment tracking, and executive summary generation. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the strategic meaning behind metric shifts, resolving conflicts between short-term conversion and long-term trust, defining the design roadmap, and communicating experience value to organizational leadership.

For example, product leaders first request an evaluation of a major user onboarding overhaul. The UX/UI designer defines which experience metrics and business KPIs must be analyzed. An AI agent then aggregates historical conversion rates, time-on-task data, and user sentiment scores, comparing the new onboarding flow against the previous baseline. The designer reviews the aggregated metrics, interprets the underlying behavioral shifts, and determines which experience improvements directly drove business value. Based on the established impact, the agent generates the executive summary and supporting data visualizations. Finally, the designer presents the strategic experience analysis and ensures that the future product roadmap remains aligned with proven user needs.

## 1.6. Working as a UX/UI Designer with Agentic AI

Agentic AI is becoming an increasingly powerful component of UX/UI design. UX/UI designers should therefore adopt and integrate AI agents as complementary participants in professional workflows rather than treating them only as occasional tools for wireframe generation, asset resizing, or accessibility scanning.

This shift does not reduce the importance of UX/UI design expertise. Human professionals remain responsible for the occupation and its corresponding responsibilities. They understand user psychology, define interaction models, establish visual hierarchies, evaluate cognitive consequences, determine acceptable usability trade-offs, interpret accessibility requirements, communicate with stakeholders, control consequential design decisions, evaluate task performance, and decide how human and agentic skills are configured and improved.

At the same time, agentic skills greatly expand the volume and variety of design information that can be processed. AI agents retrieve and organize extensive behavioral data, analyze large session recording libraries, map complex user flows, compare design alternatives, identify accessibility inconsistencies, evaluate heuristic alignment, prepare interactive prototypes and documentation, and synthesize evidence across multiple product domains at a scale difficult to achieve through direct human effort alone.

Strong UX/UI design practice is therefore not based on maximizing human work or agentic work independently. It is based on finding appropriate configurations of human skills and agentic skills for the responsibilities, tasks, subtasks, and actions being performed.

Where a human skill and an agentic skill can independently perform the same action, skills competition identifies which is better suited to the current context. Where work requires complementary capabilities, skills collaboration results in human and agentic skills being activated sequentially, iteratively, or recursively to achieve stronger task performance than either skill type achieves alone.

Task performance evaluation then determines whether selected skills and configurations satisfy predefined expectations. Successful skills are retained and reused, while inadequate skills are refined, reconfigured, or newly developed. The resulting capabilities return to an increasingly useful skill space that supports UX/UI design and, where capabilities are reusable, other occupations as well.

For a UX/UI designer, working effectively with agentic AI means treating AI as a powerful complementary capability integrated into everyday professional routines while maintaining human control of occupational responsibilities. Through appropriate skill selection, competition, collaboration, evaluation, and continuous improvement, human professionals and agentic AI work together to achieve UX/UI design that is more intuitive, accessible, scalable, adaptable, efficient, and strategically aligned.
