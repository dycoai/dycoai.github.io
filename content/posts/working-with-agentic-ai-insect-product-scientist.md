# 1. Working with Agentic AI: Insect Production Scientist

*How human skills and agentic skills are dynamically integrated and coordinated across insect production responsibilities*

## 1.1. Insect Production Scientist as an Occupation

An **insect production scientist** designs, optimizes, and scales insect mass-rearing systems by managing life cycles, formulating diets, controlling environments, and analyzing production data. This occupation connects biological sciences with industrial manufacturing by translating entomological principles into scalable processes that support sustainable protein production, organic waste conversion, and biological resource efficiency. To ensure production systems remain viable and high-yielding, the scientist must understand how insect genetics, nutritional requirements, microclimates, pathogen pressures, and facility constraints collectively shape the biological manufacturing landscape.

Typical responsibilities of an insect production scientist include:

* insect life cycle and breeding management;
* diet formulation and nutrition optimization;
* environmental control and facility operations;
* health monitoring and biosecurity management;
* production scaling and process engineering;
* data analysis and yield optimization;
* quality assurance and regulatory compliance; and
* research, experimentation, and knowledge development.

These responsibilities are fulfilled through distinct tasks and subtasks, each requiring one or more actions with specific skill requirements. Human skills and agentic skills both contribute to these responsibilities, but their levels and forms of involvement differ according to the responsibility, task, action, biological scope, operational context, system complexity, technical environment, and expected outcome.

## 1.2. Human Skills and Agentic Skills in Insect Production

Insect production increasingly integrates both **human skills** and **agentic skills**.

Human skills are reusable capabilities possessed and developed by insect production scientists. They include biological reasoning, nutritional formulation, ecological understanding, systems thinking, experimental design, observational judgment, pathology diagnosis, process engineering, contextual interpretation, and professional judgment.

Agentic skills are reusable capabilities available to AI agents through their underlying models, contextual information, instructions, constraints, tools, and computational resources. In insect production, these skills include environmental data aggregation, image-based morphometric analysis, diet composition calculation, predictive yield modeling, literature retrieval, anomaly detection, protocol generation, and sensor telemetry monitoring.

Both skill types contribute to the same occupational responsibilities, but their involvement varies by action. Defining a new rearing protocol relies primarily on human biological reasoning and ecological understanding; an AI agent then uses agentic skills to retrieve relevant entomological literature and calculate baseline nutritional requirements. In environmental control, the scientist first establishes the optimal climate parameters for a specific developmental stage, and an AI agent subsequently monitors sensor telemetry and adjusts facility systems to maintain those conditions. For health monitoring, an AI agent scans colony images for visual anomalies, while the scientist interprets the behavioral context, evaluates pathogen risks, and determines the appropriate biological response. Across these actions, human and agentic skills are activated sequentially or iteratively based on task requirements, with the human professional maintaining oversight and final judgment.

The relationship between these skills is not a fixed division where certain responsibilities permanently belong to humans and others to AI. Instead, human and agentic involvement shifts across tasks and between different actions within the same task.


## 1.3. Dynamic Human–Agentic Skill Integration and Collaboration in Insect Production

The work of an insect production scientist can be understood as a structured set of responsibilities fulfilled through tasks and subtasks, which are executed through actions requiring appropriate configurations of human and agentic skills.

An **occupation** provides the overall professional context. For an insect production scientist, this context encompasses optimizing biological manufacturing, managing mass-rearing environments, formulating nutritional substrates, governing biosecurity, and maintaining high-yield colony health.

A **responsibility** represents an expected area of professional work or an outcome that the insect production scientist accomplishes or maintains. Responsibilities such as breeding management, diet formulation, environmental control, and yield optimization each contain multiple tasks.

A **task** is a **context-dependent unit of work** used to fulfill part or all of a responsibility. When a task is too broad or complex for reliable execution, it is decomposed into smaller **subtasks**.

Tasks and subtasks are executed through one or more **actions**. Actions represent **reusable and configurable operations** such as calculating feed conversion ratios, analyzing colony morphometrics, comparing substrate nutritional profiles, evaluating climate sensor logs, identifying behavioral anomalies, or generating a rearing protocol.

Each action has **skill requirements**. These requirements describe the capabilities needed for effective and reliable execution. Depending on the action and its context, human skills, agentic skills, or an integration of both can satisfy these requirements.

The skills available for insect production reside in a **skill space** containing both human skills and agentic skills. Relevant skills in this space can serve as candidate skills for executing the actions required by a particular task. A human skill in observational judgment, for example, can serve as a candidate for diagnosing subtle colony stress, while an agentic skill in anomaly detection can serve as a candidate for flagging deviations in environmental sensor data.

Candidate skills are evaluated relative to the requirements of the action and its occupational and task context. Appropriate skills are then selected and organized into a **skill configuration** for execution. Depending on these requirements, a configuration may rely mainly on human skills, mainly on agentic skills, or on an integrated arrangement of both.

### 1.3.1. Skills Competition and Skills Collaboration

Two important relationships can occur between human skills and agentic skills when constructing a skill configuration: **skills competition** and **skills collaboration**.

**Skills competition** occurs when both a human skill and an agentic skill can satisfy the same or similar requirements for an action, but simultaneous application of both is unnecessary. Under a particular occupational and task context, one skill type can provide a better fit for the action than the other.

For example, both an insect production scientist and an AI agent can calculate the candidate macronutrient ratio for a standard rearing diet. When the nutritional requirements are well-documented, the substrate composition is explicit, and the calculation follows defined linear programming rules, the agentic skill provides fast and consistent execution. When the formulation requires balancing novel, unconventional organic waste streams with unpredictable anti-nutritional factors, the scientist's human skills provide the biological and chemical reasoning required for the action.

The purpose of skills competition is not to establish whether human or agentic skills are generally superior. Rather, it identifies which available skill provides the better fit for a particular action under particular conditions.

**Skills collaboration** occurs when one skill type alone cannot effectively satisfy the requirements of a task or its actions, or when combining complementary human and agentic skills produces stronger task performance.

Collaboration does not require human and agentic skills to operate simultaneously. Instead, they are activated at different stages of action execution in a **sequential, iterative, or recursive manner** according to evolving task requirements.

For example, an insect production scientist first identifies an unexplained drop in pupation rates within a specific rearing zone and determines which biological and environmental variables require investigation. An AI agent then examines historical climate logs, feed batch records, and colony density metrics, identifying a subtle correlation with nighttime humidity fluctuations. The scientist subsequently interprets these findings in relation to insect respiratory biology and develops several environmental adjustment alternatives. Based on the scientist's direction, the agent next simulates the energy costs and biological outcomes of these alternatives across the facility's HVAC constraints. Finally, the scientist evaluates the resulting evidence and determines which climate adjustment strategy should guide subsequent operational planning. Throughout this sequence, human and agentic skills are activated iteratively as task requirements evolve, while the scientist directs the analysis and evaluates its outcomes.

This dynamic relationship allows insect production scientists to leverage the complementary strengths of both skill types rather than relying on a permanent allocation of work.

### 1.3.2. Skill Configuration and Task Execution

A **skill configuration** specifies which human skills and agentic skills are used to execute the actions associated with a task or subtask and how those skills are coordinated.

The configuration depends on factors such as action requirements, biological complexity, substrate variability, facility constraints, expected yield, biosecurity risks, operational impact, decision consequences, stakeholder expectations, available tools, and intermediate results.

For one action, an agentic skill may independently satisfy the requirements. For another, a human skill may provide the better fit. A complex insect production task may require several configurations across multiple actions, with human and agentic skills repeatedly interacting as the task progresses.

The configuration is therefore dynamic rather than permanent. It may change when the action changes, new biological data becomes available, substrate quality shifts, an agentic result fails to satisfy expected requirements, or human interpretation identifies a new operational direction.

Throughout this process, the insect production scientist remains the professional responsible for directing and controlling the work associated with the occupation and its responsibilities. Agentic AI provides powerful complementary capabilities that expand what the professional can calculate, monitor, analyze, simulate, formulate, and communicate.

### 1.3.3. Task Performance Evaluation and Skill Space Adaptation

The performance produced through a selected skill configuration is evaluated against **predefined evaluation metrics**. These metrics determine whether the human skills, agentic skills, or their integrated configuration are sufficiently capable of performing the relevant task and its required actions.

Evaluation criteria depend on the work being performed. In insect production, they include feed conversion efficiency, biomass yield, colony survival rate, nutritional output, biological consistency, operational scalability, and other task-specific expectations.

When a selected skill or skill configuration produces performance above the defined threshold, it passes the performance evaluation and is retained for future applications under appropriate conditions. Repeated successful use provides additional evidence about where that skill or configuration performs effectively.

When performance falls below the required threshold, the corresponding skill or configuration is not reused without modification. The relevant human or agentic skills may be refined or reconfigured, existing skills can be combined differently, or new human and agentic skills may be developed or constructed from scratch when the existing skill space lacks adequate capability.

For example, an AI agent first generates predictive yield models based on standard temperature and feeding schedules, but task performance evaluation shows that it repeatedly fails to account for metabolic heat spikes generated by high-density larval clusters. The agentic skill is then refined through improved contextual instructions, integration with thermal imaging sensors, stronger biological heat-dissipation procedures, or validation against empirical density trials. The revised skill is evaluated again, and once it satisfies the required performance criteria, it is retained for appropriate future yield-prediction tasks. Human skills follow a corresponding development process: when a scientist lacks sufficient knowledge of a newly introduced pathogen affecting the colony, the relevant capability is developed through learning, collaboration, and practical experience before its performance is evaluated through subsequent work.

Refined and newly constructed skills that satisfy the required performance criteria are added to the skill space. In this way, task performance evaluation provides feedback not only for completing current work but also for continuously optimizing and expanding the available skill space.

The skill space is not constructed as an isolated collection of occupation-specific representations. Many human and agentic skills used in insect production are reusable across occupations. Biological reasoning, systems thinking, literature retrieval, predictive modeling, anomaly detection, protocol generation, and data aggregation contribute to work in traditional agriculture, aquaculture, biotechnology, food science, environmental engineering, and many other fields.

Reusable skills can therefore be represented once and configured differently according to the responsibilities, tasks, actions, and contexts of different occupations. This allows the skill space to serve a growing range of occupations without unnecessarily duplicating or exploding the number of skill representations. Occupational specialization is introduced through task context, action requirements, biological parameters, information sources, tools, constraints, and skill configurations rather than by creating a completely separate skill representation whenever the same underlying capability appears in another occupation.

The resulting process is adaptive:

**occupational responsibility → task and subtask → actions → skill requirements → candidate human and agentic skills → skill configuration → task execution → task performance evaluation → skill refinement, reconfiguration, or construction → optimized and expanded skill space**

This cycle allows human and agentic capabilities to evolve alongside the requirements of insect production work.

## 1.4. Essential Responsibilities of Insect Production Scientist

### 1.4.1. Insect Life Cycle and Breeding Management

Insect life cycle and breeding management establishes the genetic health, reproductive efficiency, and developmental stability required to maintain a robust master colony. This responsibility includes defining selective breeding objectives, tracking generation times, evaluating morphometric traits, identifying genetic bottlenecks, and determining how colony genetics should be managed over time.

Agentic skills contribute to image-based morphometric analysis, generation time tracking, pedigree mapping, and comparison of genetic lineages. Human skills remain essential for interpreting subtle behavioral mating cues, balancing genetic diversity with production traits, determining acceptable inbreeding thresholds, and defining a breeding strategy that remains biologically viable.

For example, an insect production scientist first defines the selective breeding objectives required to improve larval biomass conversion and identifies the core breeding population. An AI agent then analyzes morphometric images and developmental logs, summarizing significant trait variations and tracking generation times. The scientist interprets these findings and develops several candidate mating pairings to optimize genetic diversity. Based on the scientist's criteria, the agent next compares these pairings across projected yield, genetic distance, and reproductive viability. Finally, the scientist resolves the major trade-offs and establishes the breeding protocol that guides subsequent colony propagation.

| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| biological reasoning, genetic strategy, behavioral interpretation, trait prioritization, diversity management | morphometric analysis, generation tracking, pedigree mapping, lineage comparison, predictive modeling | The scientist defines strategic breeding direction and evaluates biological trade-offs, while the AI agent organizes morphometric evidence, tracks lineages, and compares pairing alternatives. |

### 1.4.2. Diet Formulation and Nutrition Optimization

Diet formulation and nutrition optimization translates organic substrates and nutritional requirements into structured feed recipes that maximize insect growth and biological efficiency. This responsibility includes analyzing substrate composition, defining nutritional targets, formulating feed blends, identifying anti-nutritional factors, and establishing diet protocols that connect raw materials with insect metabolic needs.

Agentic skills are well suited to calculating linear programming formulations, mapping substrate nutritional profiles, comparing historical feed conversion ratios, and identifying cost-optimal ingredient blends. Human skills contribute to determining the palatability of novel substrates, resolving ambiguous digestibility issues, defining future-state nutritional buffers, and deciding how substrate variability should influence feeding strategies.

For example, an AI agent first extracts the nutritional profiles of available organic byproducts and prepares a preliminary cost-optimized diet formulation. The insect production scientist reviews the formulation and identifies areas where high fiber content or specific anti-nutritional factors will impair larval digestion. The scientist then restructures the recipe around the biological constraints of the insect's gut microbiome. Based on the revised structure, the agent recalculates the macronutrient balances and maps the required preprocessing steps for the raw materials. Finally, the scientist validates the resulting diet protocol and determines which feeding trials require immediate operational attention.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| nutritional reasoning, digestive biology, substrate evaluation, palatability judgment, metabolic strategy | formulation calculation, profile mapping, cost optimization, ratio comparison, protocol documentation | The AI agent accelerates calculation and optimization of substrate data, while the scientist defines the biological constraints and determines the nutritional strategy for the colony. |

### 1.4.3. Environmental Control and Facility Operations

Environmental control and facility operations maintains a coherent view of the microclimates, lighting, and airflow systems used across the rearing facility. This responsibility includes tracking thermal dynamics, identifying humidity bottlenecks, examining metabolic heat accumulation, evaluating climate uniformity, and establishing environmental set-points.

Agentic skills efficiently classify large sensor datasets, extract thermal profiles, identify airflow dead zones, map climate dependencies, summarize energy consumption, and identify candidates for HVAC optimization. Human skills remain essential for understanding insect stress responses, evaluating the biological consequences of microclimate fluctuations, determining whether apparent energy savings represent genuine welfare improvements, and establishing environmental priorities.

For example, the insect production scientist first defines the criteria for reviewing the facility's climate performance, including developmental speed, stress indicators, and energy efficiency. An AI agent then classifies the sensor telemetry against these criteria and identifies potential areas of thermal stratification and humidity loss. The scientist reviews the identified candidates and distinguishes genuine biological stress zones from cases where sensor drift creates misleading data artifacts. The agent next analyzes the HVAC adjustment implications for the selected zones. Finally, the scientist establishes recommendations for set-point recalibration, airflow redirection, or insulation upgrades.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| stress physiology, microclimate governance, biological consequence evaluation, facility strategy, operational judgment | telemetry classification, thermal profiling, dead-zone detection, dependency mapping, energy analysis | The AI agent systematically examines large environmental datasets and identifies potential optimization opportunities, while the scientist determines which climate changes are biologically appropriate within the wider rearing context. |

### 1.4.4. Health Monitoring and Biosecurity Management

Health monitoring and biosecurity management ensures that mass-rearing colonies remain protected from pathogens, parasites, and biological contamination. This responsibility includes defining biosecurity protocols, tracking mortality baselines, identifying disease vectors, evaluating sanitation effectiveness, and guiding facility operations toward appropriate biological containment.

Agentic skills support computer-vision-based anomaly detection, mortality curve tracking, pathogen literature scanning, sanitation schedule generation, and biosecurity audit preparation. Human skills remain essential for interpreting complex pathology symptoms, evaluating justified exceptions to sanitation rules, resolving competing operational priorities, and controlling consequential quarantine decisions.

For example, a facility operator first submits a report of unusual larval lethargy and discoloration. The insect production scientist establishes the relevant pathology indicators, biosecurity thresholds, and investigation criteria. An AI agent then analyzes colony camera feeds and environmental logs, identifying areas of localized mortality, potential vector entry points, and sanitation gaps. The scientist reviews these findings and determines which issues represent a systemic pathogen outbreak requiring immediate quarantine. Based on the additional biological evidence, the agent performs another facility-wide risk comparison. Finally, the scientist evaluates the complete evidence and determines whether the affected zone should be culled, treated, or isolated.

### 1.4.5. Production Scaling and Process Engineering

Production scaling and process engineering ensures that laboratory-proven rearing methods translate effectively into industrial-scale biological manufacturing. This responsibility includes defining scaling parameters, evaluating automation alternatives, establishing mass-rearing logistics, guiding equipment design, and ensuring that biological realities remain aligned with mechanical engineering constraints.

Agentic skills assist with bioprocess simulation, equipment sizing calculations, logistics mapping, spatial utilization modeling, and automation scenario evaluation. Human skills remain essential for determining the biological limits of mechanization, evaluating the trade-offs between automation and manual handling, understanding the operational consequences of scale-up, and selecting engineering directions that remain consistent with insect welfare.

For example, the insect production scientist first defines the biological limits, density thresholds, and handling requirements for a new automated harvesting system. An AI agent then compares several mechanical conveying arrangements and summarizes their spatial footprints, estimated throughput, and potential physical impact on the larvae. The scientist reviews the alternatives and identifies where certain mechanical designs create unacceptable biological stress. Based on the scientist's revised constraints, the agent generates an additional conveyor scenario and updates the bioprocess simulation. Finally, the scientist selects the engineering direction that best satisfies both biological and operational requirements and establishes the corresponding scaling guidance.

### 1.4.6. Data Analysis and Yield Optimization

Data analysis and yield optimization establishes how biological inputs, environmental variables, and harvesting outputs should be tracked, correlated, and optimized across the facility. This responsibility includes defining feed conversion ratio (FCR) models, analyzing biomass accumulation curves, identifying yield bottlenecks, aligning production metrics with biological baselines, and ensuring that operational decisions support maximum efficiency.

Agentic skills efficiently aggregate sensor and harvest data, identify statistical correlations, generate yield dashboards, detect operational anomalies, and analyze consistency across different rearing batches. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the root biological drivers of yield variances, determining appropriate operational interventions, resolving cross-variable conflicts, and ensuring that yield-maximization decisions do not degrade long-term colony health.

For example, an AI agent first aggregates a month of harvest data against feeding and climate logs, identifying specific batches where the feed conversion ratio deviated significantly from the biological baseline. The insect production scientist reviews the resulting analytical landscape and determines which deviations require immediate dietary adjustments and which indicate underlying genetic drift. The scientist then defines the corrective biological parameters. Based on this direction, the agent recalculates the projected yield impacts and generates the operational variance reports. Finally, the scientist validates the biological impact and presents the yield optimization strategy to facility leadership.

### 1.4.7. Quality Assurance and Regulatory Compliance

Quality assurance and regulatory compliance ensures that insect production incorporates appropriate food safety principles, heavy metal monitoring, and regulatory expectations. This responsibility includes evaluating biological risks, defining testing frequencies, examining the implications of substrate contamination, identifying control gaps, and ensuring that production decisions remain compatible with agricultural and food safety regulations.

Agentic skills support laboratory result aggregation, regulatory literature comparison, contamination tracking, gap identification, and consistency checking across testing protocols. Human skills remain essential for interpreting complex toxicological thresholds, defining acceptable biological risk, resolving conflicts between production speed and safety testing, and determining the appropriate quality assurance response.

For example, the insect production scientist first establishes the safety thresholds and testing requirements relevant to a new organic waste substrate. An AI agent then compares the incoming substrate certificates of analysis with established regulatory limits, tracking heavy metals and pesticide residues, and identifies potential compliance gaps. The scientist reviews the findings and determines which trace elements represent material biological risk and which fall within acceptable regulatory margins. Based on the scientist's decisions, the agent updates the risk matrix and evaluates the revised sourcing strategy against the relevant safety standards. Finally, the scientist assesses the residual risk and determines whether the substrate satisfies the organization's quality acceptance criteria.

### 1.4.8. Research, Experimentation, and Knowledge Development

Research, experimentation, and knowledge development connects biological discovery with operational application, ensuring that new entomological insights are systematically tested and integrated into the mass-rearing framework. This responsibility includes defining experimental hypotheses, designing controlled trials, analyzing statistical significance, maintaining research roadmaps, and adjusting protocols as biological understanding evolves.

Agentic skills support literature summarization, experimental design drafting, statistical power calculation, data extraction, and preparation of research documentation. Human skills remain essential for generating novel biological hypotheses, interpreting unexpected experimental outcomes, explaining biological trade-offs, and maintaining alignment between scientific discovery and commercial production realities.

For example, research leadership first requests an investigation into the impact of specific light spectrums on insect reproductive output. The insect production scientist defines which biological variables and control groups must be established. An AI agent then maps the existing literature, drafts the experimental design, and calculates the required statistical power and sample sizes. The scientist reviews the design, discusses the biological trade-offs with the facility operations team, and determines which environmental controls are feasible without disrupting mainline production. Based on the agreed direction, the agent generates the data collection templates and updates the research documentation. Finally, the scientist validates the experimental protocol and ensures that the research remains aligned with organizational strategic objectives.

## 1.6. Working as an Insect Production Scientist with Agentic AI

Agentic AI is becoming an increasingly powerful component of insect production. Insect production scientists should therefore adopt and integrate AI agents as complementary participants in professional workflows rather than treating them only as occasional tools for data aggregation, literature retrieval, or basic calculation.

This shift does not reduce the importance of entomological and biological expertise. Human professionals remain responsible for the occupation and its corresponding responsibilities. They understand insect biology, define breeding and nutritional strategies, establish environmental parameters, evaluate cross-system biological consequences, determine acceptable welfare and yield trade-offs, interpret biosecurity and regulatory requirements, communicate with stakeholders, control consequential production decisions, evaluate task performance, and decide how human and agentic skills are configured and improved.

At the same time, agentic skills greatly expand the volume and variety of biological and operational information that can be processed. AI agents retrieve and organize extensive entomological literature, analyze large sensor and morphometric datasets, map facility microclimates, compare diet formulations, identify biological inconsistencies, evaluate compliance alignment, prepare experimental protocols and documentation, and synthesize evidence across multiple rearing zones at a scale difficult to achieve through direct human effort alone.

Strong insect production practice is therefore not based on maximizing human work or agentic work independently. It is based on finding appropriate configurations of human skills and agentic skills for the responsibilities, tasks, subtasks, and actions being performed.

Where a human skill and an agentic skill can independently perform the same action, skills competition identifies which is better suited to the current context. Where work requires complementary capabilities, skills collaboration results in human and agentic skills being activated sequentially, iteratively, or recursively to achieve stronger task performance than either skill type achieves alone.

Task performance evaluation then determines whether selected skills and configurations satisfy predefined expectations. Successful skills are retained and reused, while inadequate skills are refined, reconfigured, or newly developed. The resulting capabilities return to an increasingly useful skill space that supports insect production and, where capabilities are reusable, other occupations as well.

For an insect production scientist, working effectively with agentic AI means treating AI as a powerful complementary capability integrated into everyday professional routines while maintaining human control of occupational responsibilities. Through appropriate skill selection, competition, collaboration, evaluation, and continuous improvement, human professionals and agentic AI work together to achieve insect production that is more biologically efficient, scalable, adaptable, sustainable, and scientifically rigorous.
