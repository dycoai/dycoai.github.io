# 1. Working with Agentic AI: Management Analyst

_How human skills and agentic skills can be dynamically integrated and coordinated across management analysis responsibilities_

## 1.1. Management Analysts as an Occupation

A **management analyst** is a professional who studies organizational problems, processes, structures, information, and performance to identify opportunities for improvement and recommend changes that can help organizations operate more effectively and efficiently. The occupation connects organizational analysis with problem solving, data analysis, process improvement, systems design, management advisory work, organizational change, and performance evaluation.

Management analysis extends beyond identifying inefficiencies or preparing recommendations. A management analyst must understand how organizational objectives, work processes, personnel, information flows, financial conditions, operating procedures, technology, management practices, and organizational constraints interact. The analyst examines current conditions, identifies problems or opportunities, develops possible solutions, communicates recommendations, and helps determine whether implemented changes produce the intended results.

Typical responsibilities of management analysts include:

- organizational studies and current-state assessment;
    
- business, financial, and organizational performance analysis;
    
- process, system, and organizational improvement design;
    
- management recommendations and strategic advisory support;
    
- implementation, change coordination, and effectiveness evaluation; and
    
- procedures, documentation, records, and knowledge transfer.
    

These responsibilities are fulfilled through different tasks and subtasks, and each task may require multiple actions with different skill requirements. Human skills and agentic skills can both contribute to performing these responsibilities, but their levels and forms of involvement may differ according to the nature of the organizational problem, task ambiguity, information quality, stakeholder involvement, analytical complexity, organizational consequences, and expected outcome.

## 1.2. Human Skills and Agentic Skills in Management Analysis

Management analysis increasingly involves both **human skills** and **agentic skills**.

Human skills are reusable capabilities possessed and developed by management analysts. They may include organizational analysis, analytical reasoning, systems thinking, financial reasoning, process analysis, interviewing, observation, stakeholder communication, problem solving, facilitation, strategic reasoning, contextual interpretation, change management, and professional judgment.

Agentic skills are reusable capabilities available to AI agents through their underlying models, contextual information, instructions, constraints, tools, and computational resources. In management analysis, these skills may include information retrieval, document extraction, classification, summarization, data analysis, financial comparison, trend analysis, process mapping, workflow comparison, scenario generation, report drafting, consistency checking, recommendation support, and tool use.

Both skill types can contribute to the same occupational responsibilities, but their involvement levels vary. Organizational diagnosis may rely heavily on human interaction, observation, and contextual interpretation while using agentic skills to organize and compare evidence. Large-scale analysis of operational or financial information may involve high agentic participation while requiring human judgment to determine what identified patterns mean. Process redesign may combine agentic generation and comparison of alternatives with human understanding of organizational feasibility and stakeholder consequences.

The useful relationship is therefore not a fixed division in which certain responsibilities permanently belong to humans and others belong to AI. Human and agentic involvement can change from one task to another and even between different actions within the same task.

## 1.3. Dynamic Human–Agentic Skill Integration and Collaboration in Management Analysis

Management analysis can be understood as an occupation in which responsibilities are fulfilled through tasks and subtasks, tasks and subtasks are executed through actions, and actions require appropriate configurations of human and agentic skills.

An **occupation** provides the overall professional context. For management analysis, this context includes studying organizational operations, identifying problems and improvement opportunities, evaluating alternatives, recommending changes, supporting implementation, and helping management improve organizational performance.

A **responsibility** represents an expected area of professional work or an outcome that the management analyst is expected to accomplish or maintain. Responsibilities such as studying organizational problems, analyzing performance, redesigning procedures, or advising management may each contain multiple tasks.

A **task** is a context-dependent unit of work used to fulfill part or all of a responsibility. When a task is too broad or complex for reliable execution, it may be decomposed into smaller **subtasks**.

Tasks and subtasks are executed through one or more **actions**. Actions represent reusable and configurable operations such as retrieving information, interviewing personnel, examining reports, observing work processes, calculating performance measures, comparing alternatives, mapping workflows, identifying causes, developing recommendations, preparing documentation, or evaluating an implemented change.

Each action has **skill requirements**. These requirements describe the capabilities needed for the action to be executed effectively and reliably. Depending on the action and its context, the requirements may be satisfied by human skills, agentic skills, or an integration of both.

The skills available for management analysis reside in a **skill space** containing both human skills and agentic skills. Some skills in this space become candidate skills for executing the actions required by a particular task. A human skill in organizational interpretation, for example, may be a candidate for understanding why a work process operates in a particular way, while an agentic skill in process comparison may be a candidate for identifying differences among documented workflows.

The candidate skills are considered relative to the requirements of the action and the occupational and task context. Appropriate skills are then selected and organized into a **skill configuration** for execution. A configuration may rely mainly on human skills, mainly on agentic skills, or on an integrated arrangement of both.

### 1.3.1. Skills Competition and Skills Collaboration

Two important relationships may occur between human skills and agentic skills when a skill configuration is constructed: **skills competition** and **skills collaboration**.

**Skills competition** occurs when both a human skill and an agentic skill can satisfy the same or similar requirements for an action, but both do not need to be applied simultaneously. Under a particular occupational and task context, one may be better suited than the other.

For example, both a management analyst and an AI agent may be capable of comparing two documented operating procedures and identifying differences. When the procedures are complete, standardized, and structured, an agentic skill may perform the comparison more rapidly and consistently. When the differences depend on undocumented work practices or informal organizational relationships, the analyst's contextual understanding may provide the better fit.

The purpose of skills competition is therefore not to establish whether human or agentic skills are generally superior. It is to identify which available skill provides the better fit for a particular action under particular conditions.

**Skills collaboration** occurs when the requirements of a task or its actions cannot be satisfied effectively by one skill type alone, or when combining complementary human and agentic skills is expected to produce better task performance.

Collaboration does not require human and agentic skills to operate at exactly the same time. They may be activated at different stages of action execution in a **sequential, iterative, or recursive manner**.

For example, an analyst may interview personnel to understand a recurring operational problem. An AI agent may organize the interview notes, compare them with process documentation and performance data, and identify possible causes. The analyst may return to personnel to investigate one of those causes, after which the agent performs additional analysis. The analyst then uses the resulting evidence to refine the diagnosis and develop possible solutions.

This dynamic relationship allows management analysts to exploit the complementary strengths of both skill types rather than relying on a permanent allocation of work.

### 1.3.2. Skill Configuration and Task Execution

A skill configuration specifies which human skills and agentic skills will be used to execute the actions associated with a task or subtask and how those skills will be coordinated.

The configuration depends on factors such as problem ambiguity, organizational complexity, information quality, stakeholder diversity, analytical requirements, available tools, expected reliability, implementation consequences, time constraints, and intermediate results.

For one action, an agentic skill may independently satisfy the requirements. For another, a human skill may be selected instead. A complicated organizational study may require several configurations across multiple actions, with human and agentic skills repeatedly interacting as the work progresses.

The configuration is therefore dynamic rather than permanent. It may change when new organizational information becomes available, an initial assumption proves incorrect, stakeholder evidence conflicts, an agentic result fails validation, or human interpretation identifies a different direction for the study.

Throughout this process, the management analyst remains the professional responsible for promoting and controlling the work associated with the occupation and its responsibilities. Agentic AI provides powerful complementary capabilities that can expand what the professional can retrieve, analyze, compare, model, document, evaluate, and communicate.

### 1.3.3. Task Performance Evaluation and Skill Space Adaptation

The performance produced through a selected skill configuration must be evaluated against **predefined evaluation metrics**. These metrics determine whether the human skills, agentic skills, or their integrated configuration are sufficiently capable of performing the relevant task and its required actions.

The evaluation criteria depend on the work being performed. In management analysis, they may include analytical accuracy, completeness, consistency, problem relevance, recommendation quality, financial validity, process efficiency, stakeholder acceptance, implementation feasibility, documentation quality, timeliness, and reliability.

When a selected skill or skill configuration produces performance above the defined threshold, it passes the performance evaluation and can be retained for future applications under appropriate conditions. Repeated successful use provides additional evidence about where that skill or configuration can be applied effectively.

When performance falls below the required threshold, the corresponding skill or configuration should not simply be reused without modification. Human or agentic skills may be refined or reconfigured. Existing skills may also be combined differently, or new human and agentic skills may be developed or constructed when the existing skill space does not provide an adequate capability.

For example, if an agentic process-analysis skill consistently identifies formal workflow bottlenecks but fails to account for important informal handoffs, the skill may be refined with additional interview evidence, organizational context, or a human-review stage. If the analyst lacks sufficient knowledge of a specialized operational area, the corresponding human skill may be developed through research, training, and practical experience.

Refined and newly constructed skills that satisfy the required performance criteria can be added to the skill space. Task performance evaluation therefore provides feedback not only for completing the current management analysis but also for continuously optimizing and expanding available capabilities.

The skill space should not be constructed as an isolated collection of occupation-specific representations. Many capabilities used in management analysis are reusable across occupations. Information retrieval, interviewing, data analysis, financial reasoning, process mapping, comparison, report drafting, communication, scenario analysis, facilitation, and tool use may also contribute to business analysis, operations analysis, project management, consulting, financial analysis, human resources, and other professional work.

Reusable skills can therefore be represented once and configured differently according to the responsibilities, tasks, actions, and contexts of different occupations. Occupational specialization can be introduced through organizational context, analytical requirements, stakeholder information, tools, constraints, parameters, and skill configurations rather than unnecessarily creating a separate representation whenever the same underlying capability appears elsewhere.

The resulting process is adaptive:

**occupational responsibility → task and subtask → actions → skill requirements → candidate human and agentic skills → skill configuration → task execution → task performance evaluation → skill refinement, reconfiguration, or construction → optimized and expanded skill space**

This cycle allows human and agentic capabilities to evolve together with the requirements of management analysis.

## 1.4. Essential Responsibilities of Management Analysts

### 1.4.1. Organizational Studies and Current-State Assessment

Organizational studies and current-state assessment establishes how an organization, business unit, or operational function currently works and identifies problems, constraints, or opportunities that require further analysis. The responsibility may involve planning organizational studies, gathering information, interviewing personnel, observing work activities, examining information flows, understanding organizational structures, and documenting current operating conditions.

Human skills are especially important for conducting interviews, interpreting organizational behavior, understanding informal work practices, recognizing stakeholder interests, and distinguishing stated procedures from actual operating conditions. Agentic skills can complement this work by retrieving information, organizing interview material, comparing documents, summarizing observations, identifying recurring themes, and integrating evidence from multiple sources.

For example, management may report that an operating unit is experiencing persistent delays. The analyst interviews personnel and observes how work moves through the unit. An AI agent organizes the interview notes, compares them with documented procedures, and identifies several stages where actual practice differs from the formal process. The analyst determines that one difference appears important and gathers additional evidence. The agent then incorporates the new information into the current-state analysis, allowing the diagnosis to develop iteratively.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|
|---|---|---|
|organizational observation, interviewing, systems thinking, contextual interpretation, problem framing|information retrieval, document analysis, interview summarization, evidence comparison, theme identification|The analyst investigates how the organization actually operates while the AI agent organizes and compares evidence from documents, interviews, observations, and other sources to support a structured current-state assessment.|

### 1.4.2. Business, Financial, and Organizational Performance Analysis

Business, financial, and organizational performance analysis examines quantitative and qualitative information to identify patterns, performance gaps, costs, resource issues, and possible causes of organizational problems.

The responsibility may involve analyzing operating data, financial information, workforce information, productivity measures, service performance, cost structures, capacity, workload, or other indicators relevant to organizational effectiveness.

Agentic skills can contribute extensively through data extraction, calculation, financial comparison, trend analysis, anomaly detection, segmentation, visualization, and repeated analysis of large information sets. Human skills remain necessary for defining meaningful measures, interpreting patterns in context, determining whether relationships are significant, and avoiding unsupported conclusions.

For example, an AI agent may analyze operating and financial information and identify that a unit's cost per completed transaction has increased while overall workload has remained relatively stable. The analyst examines the result and asks the agent to separate staffing, technology, rework, and external service costs. The additional analysis shows that repeated rework accounts for much of the increase. The analyst then connects this finding with evidence from the current-state study to identify a likely process problem.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|  
|analytical reasoning, financial interpretation, performance-measure design, contextual reasoning, causal judgment|data extraction, calculation, financial analysis, trend detection, anomaly identification, visualization|The AI agent expands the scale and speed of performance analysis while the analyst determines which measures matter, interprets relationships, and connects quantitative findings with organizational conditions.|

### 1.4.3. Process, System, and Organizational Improvement Design

Process, system, and organizational improvement design develops alternative methods, procedures, workflows, information arrangements, or organizational changes intended to improve efficiency and effectiveness.

The responsibility may involve simplifying work, redesigning processes, improving information flows, changing forms or reporting procedures, modifying systems, reallocating activities, clarifying roles, or developing alternative operating methods.

Agentic skills can support process mapping, workflow comparison, bottleneck identification, alternative generation, dependency analysis, simulation, documentation review, and structured comparison of possible improvements. Human skills are especially important for understanding organizational feasibility, recognizing hidden dependencies, considering employee and stakeholder impacts, and determining whether a proposed improvement addresses the underlying problem.

For example, an existing process may require the same information to be entered and reviewed several times. An AI agent can map the workflow, identify duplicated steps, and generate several simplified alternatives. The analyst examines the proposals and recognizes that one repeated review exists because of an important control requirement. The analyst retains the control while redesigning the surrounding process, and the agent then models the revised workflow and compares it with the current state.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|  
|process analysis, systems thinking, organizational design, feasibility judgment, improvement reasoning|process mapping, workflow comparison, bottleneck analysis, alternative generation, dependency analysis|The AI agent identifies structural inefficiencies and produces alternative designs while the analyst evaluates why existing controls and practices exist and determines which improvements are organizationally appropriate.|

### 1.4.4. Management Recommendations and Strategic Advisory Support

Management recommendations and strategic advisory support converts organizational analysis into actionable recommendations that help managers decide how problems should be addressed and how operations, systems, policies, or organizational arrangements should change.

The responsibility includes evaluating alternatives, considering costs and benefits, assessing organizational implications, identifying risks, preparing recommendations, discussing business strategies or practices, and communicating findings through written reports, presentations, and consultations.

Agentic skills can support alternative comparison, financial calculation, scenario analysis, evidence synthesis, risk organization, report drafting, and presentation preparation. Human skills remain central to evaluating strategic fit, balancing competing organizational interests, challenging assumptions, understanding management priorities, and making defensible recommendations.

For example, an analyst may develop three possible approaches for addressing an operational problem. An AI agent compares projected costs, implementation requirements, expected performance improvements, and major risks for each approach. The analyst recognizes that the lowest-cost option would create a significant dependency on another organizational unit and asks the agent to model that dependency under several scenarios. The analyst then weighs the expanded evidence and prepares a recommendation for management.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|  
|strategic reasoning, recommendation development, cost-benefit judgment, stakeholder understanding, professional judgment|alternative comparison, financial calculation, scenario analysis, evidence synthesis, report drafting|The AI agent increases the breadth and consistency of alternative analysis while the analyst evaluates organizational implications and determines which recommendation best addresses management objectives.|

### 1.4.5. Implementation, Change Coordination, and Effectiveness Evaluation

Implementation, change coordination, and effectiveness evaluation supports the transition from an approved recommendation to a functioning organizational change and determines whether the change produces the intended results.

The responsibility may include coordinating with personnel, clarifying new procedures, identifying implementation issues, monitoring adoption, evaluating performance after implementation, gathering feedback, and recommending further adjustments when a new system or procedure does not work as expected.

Agentic skills can support implementation tracking, issue classification, progress monitoring, performance comparison, feedback summarization, change-impact analysis, and detection of deviations from expected outcomes. Human skills are especially important for coordinating stakeholders, resolving resistance or misunderstandings, interpreting unexpected behavior, and determining what corrective response is appropriate.

For example, a newly implemented procedure may reduce average processing time but create an unexpected increase in errors. An AI agent can compare pre- and post-implementation performance, identify where errors are concentrated, and summarize user feedback. The analyst discusses the issue with affected personnel and determines that one step in the new procedure is being interpreted inconsistently. The procedure is clarified, additional guidance is introduced, and the agent continues monitoring performance to determine whether the corrective action works.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|  
|change coordination, stakeholder communication, implementation judgment, problem solving, effectiveness evaluation|progress monitoring, performance comparison, issue classification, feedback summarization, change-impact analysis|The AI agent continuously measures implementation results and organizes emerging issues while the analyst coordinates affected personnel, interprets unexpected outcomes, and controls corrective action.|

### 1.4.6. Procedures, Documentation, Records, and Knowledge Transfer

Procedures, documentation, records, and knowledge transfer ensures that organizational improvements are supported by clear forms, reports, procedures, records, instructions, and training materials that allow new practices to be applied consistently.

The responsibility may include reviewing and redesigning forms and reports, preparing procedures and manuals, maintaining documentation, developing records-management practices, preparing training materials, and helping personnel learn new systems, procedures, forms, or equipment.

Agentic skills can support document generation, content comparison, consistency checking, records classification, information retrieval, procedural drafting, training-material generation, and maintenance of structured knowledge resources. Human skills remain important for determining what employees need to understand, ensuring that documentation reflects actual work, communicating changes, training personnel, and evaluating whether new materials support correct performance.

For example, an organizational redesign may introduce a new reporting procedure. An AI agent can draft the procedure, compare it with related policies, generate an updated form, and produce an initial training guide. The analyst reviews the materials with users and learns that one instruction is unclear in practice. The analyst revises the operating logic, the agent updates all related materials consistently, and the analyst uses the revised materials to support training and adoption.

|Human skills|Agentic skills|Integrated collaboration|  
|procedural judgment, communication, training, records understanding, user-centered interpretation|document generation, consistency checking, records classification, content comparison, training-material drafting|The AI agent accelerates creation and maintenance of procedures and knowledge materials while the analyst ensures that documentation reflects real organizational needs and that personnel can apply the new practices effectively.|

## 1.5. Changing Human and Agentic Involvement Across Management Analysis Responsibilities

Human skills and agentic skills are involved throughout management analysis, but their relative involvement differs across responsibilities and may change during task execution.

|Responsibility|Typical human involvement|Typical agentic involvement|Typical integration|
|---|---|---|---|
|Organizational studies and current-state assessment|Very high|High|Human interviewing, observation, and contextual interpretation supported by agentic evidence organization and comparison.|
|Business, financial, and organizational performance analysis|High|Very high|Agentic data and financial analysis combined with human interpretation and causal reasoning.|
|Process, system, and organizational improvement design|Very high|Very high|Agentic process modeling and alternative generation combined with human systems reasoning and feasibility judgment.|
|Management recommendations and strategic advisory support|Very high|Very high|Agentic comparison, scenario analysis, and evidence synthesis combined with human strategic judgment and recommendation development.|
|Implementation, change coordination, and effectiveness evaluation|Very high|Very high|Agentic monitoring and performance evaluation combined with human stakeholder coordination and corrective decisions.|
|Procedures, documentation, records, and knowledge transfer|Very high|High|Agentic documentation and information-management support combined with human procedural judgment, communication, and training.|

These involvement levels should not be interpreted as permanent allocations. A single responsibility may contain actions in which human and agentic skills compete as alternative capabilities and other actions in which they collaborate sequentially, iteratively, or recursively.

The practical objective is to construct skill configurations that use the most suitable available capabilities for each part of the work and to revise those configurations when task performance evaluation shows that another arrangement could perform more effectively or reliably.

## 1.6. Working as a Management Analyst with Agentic AI

Agentic AI is becoming an increasingly powerful and promising capability for management analysis. Management analysts should therefore be prepared to accept and embrace AI agents as complementary participants in professional workflows rather than treating them only as occasional tools for research, data analysis, or document preparation.

This does not reduce the importance of management analysis expertise. Human professionals remain responsible for the occupation and its corresponding responsibilities. They understand organizational objectives, observe how organizations actually operate, interpret stakeholder interests, diagnose problems, evaluate organizational consequences, develop recommendations, coordinate change, and determine whether improvements produce acceptable results.

At the same time, agentic skills can substantially expand the amount and variety of organizational information that can be processed. AI agents can analyze large collections of documents and operational records, compare financial and performance information, map workflows, identify repeated patterns, generate alternative procedures, model scenarios, prepare reports and documentation, and continuously monitor implementation outcomes at a scale difficult to achieve through direct human effort alone.

The strongest management analysis practice is therefore not based on maximizing human work or maximizing agentic work independently. It is based on finding appropriate configurations of human skills and agentic skills for the responsibilities, tasks, subtasks, and actions being performed.

Where a human skill and an agentic skill can independently perform the same action, skills competition can help identify which capability is better suited to the current context. Where organizational problems require complementary capabilities, skills collaboration can activate human and agentic skills sequentially, iteratively, or recursively to achieve stronger task performance.

Task performance evaluation then determines whether the selected skills and configurations satisfy predefined expectations for analytical quality, organizational relevance, feasibility, efficiency, stakeholder usefulness, implementation effectiveness, documentation quality, and reliability. Successful capabilities can be retained and reused, while inadequate capabilities can be refined, reconfigured, combined differently, or newly developed. Reusable capabilities can subsequently contribute not only to management analysis but also to other occupations requiring related analytical, process, communication, organizational, and decision-support skills.

For management analysts, working effectively with agentic AI therefore means treating AI as a powerful complementary capability that can be integrated into everyday professional routines while maintaining human control of occupational responsibilities. Through appropriate skill selection, competition, collaboration, evaluation, and continuous improvement, management analysts and agentic AI can work together to produce organizational analysis and improvement that are more comprehensive, efficient, scalable, adaptive, and reliable.
