The increasing use of **Agentic AI** in workplaces does not simply create a fixed division in which some activities belong permanently to humans while others are permanently assigned to AI agents. A more appropriate way to understand this development is as a continuously evolving combination of **human skills** and **agentic skills**. Human skills include the knowledge, judgment, experience, communication abilities, creativity, domain understanding, interpersonal capabilities, and practical competencies that people apply to their work. Agentic skills refer to reusable AI-supported capabilities through which an AI agent can perform particular tasks or subtasks, such as retrieving information, analyzing documents, generating content, writing or reviewing code, comparing alternatives, evaluating outputs, coordinating workflows, or interacting with available tools. These two categories can overlap substantially. A task that initially requires extensive human participation may later become increasingly supported by agentic skills, while tasks performed largely by AI agents may still require human judgment when objectives, constraints, risks, or circumstances change. The important question is therefore not simply whether a particular task should be performed by a human or an AI agent, but how human and agentic capabilities can be combined appropriately for the particular task, environment, and point in time.

## The Boundary Between Human and Agentic Skills Is Continuously Changing

The boundary between human skills and agentic skills should be regarded as **dynamic rather than fixed**. It can change as AI models improve, new agentic skills become available, workplace requirements evolve, organizational policies change, new information becomes available, and the state of an ongoing task develops. The appropriate division of work may even change during the execution of a single task. An agent may initially collect and organize information, after which a human evaluates uncertain evidence and determines the direction of the work; the agent may then continue by generating alternatives, implementing the selected approach, or evaluating intermediate results. If those results reveal unexpected problems, missing information, conflicting requirements, or new opportunities, responsibilities may shift again. Consequently, the integration of human and agentic skills is better understood as an adaptive allocation of capabilities in response to the **changing environment, current task state, intermediate execution results, available resources, and evolving contextual information**.

## Human Participation Remains an Active Part of Agentic Work

The availability of increasingly capable agentic skills does not necessarily imply that people should transfer entire jobs or workflows to AI agents. Complete delegation may be inappropriate when work involves unclear objectives, significant consequences, interpersonal relationships, organizational accountability, contextual knowledge, value judgments, or circumstances that cannot be adequately represented in the information available to an AI system. Human participation can therefore remain important not only as final approval but throughout the execution process. People may define objectives, establish constraints, provide contextual knowledge, select or revise approaches, inspect intermediate results, identify errors, resolve ambiguities, evaluate trade-offs, communicate with other people, and determine whether an outcome is suitable for its intended purpose. In this sense, human involvement should not be reduced to supervising an otherwise autonomous system. It can function as an integral source of knowledge, judgment, direction, evaluation, and adaptation within a broader human–agent workflow.

## Agentic Applications Should Fully Utilize the Parametric Capabilities of Large Models

At the same time, maintaining meaningful human participation should not result in unnecessarily restricting what capable AI models can contribute. Modern large models, particularly **large language models (LLMs)**, provide increasingly powerful parametric capabilities for language comprehension, reasoning, decision support, coding, generation, evaluation, information synthesis, and related forms of knowledge work. Agentic applications can make these capabilities substantially more useful by connecting them to tasks, tools, information sources, intermediate states, and iterative workflows rather than using the model only for isolated question answering or content generation. An agent can interpret a goal, determine relevant subtasks, retrieve necessary information, select available skills or tools, generate intermediate outputs, evaluate those outputs, respond to changing conditions, and continue working toward the intended objective. The practical objective should therefore be to **maximize the useful contribution of large-model capabilities while preserving human participation wherever human knowledge, responsibility, judgment, or interaction provides additional value**.

## Human–Agent Collaboration Should Adapt During Task Execution

Effective integration can consequently involve responsibilities moving repeatedly between humans and AI agents instead of following a predetermined sequence of “human work” followed by “AI work,” or the reverse. At one stage, a human may provide a broad objective while an agent decomposes it into manageable subtasks. At another stage, the agent may produce several alternatives and ask the human to determine which better reflects organizational priorities. After that decision, the agent may execute a substantial portion of the remaining work, evaluate the result, and identify issues requiring further human consideration. The human may then introduce new requirements that cause the agent to revise earlier work. Such interaction creates an **adaptive human–agent collaboration loop** in which task allocation is repeatedly reconsidered according to current conditions. The most effective arrangement is therefore not necessarily the one that maximizes automation, nor the one that preserves the largest possible amount of human work, but the one that continually assigns activities according to the capabilities, limitations, information, and responsibilities present at each stage.

## Workplace Roles Can Evolve Around Human–Agent Capability Portfolios

This changing relationship can also alter how workplace roles are defined. Traditional job descriptions often organize work around relatively stable collections of human responsibilities, whereas Agentic AI makes it increasingly possible to view a role as a **changing portfolio of human skills and agentic skills**. An employee may personally perform some activities, perform others with AI assistance, delegate particular subtasks to specialized agents, coordinate several agentic workflows, and directly intervene when situations require human expertise or accountability. As agentic capabilities improve, the composition of this portfolio can change without necessarily eliminating the broader occupational role. Some routine activities may become highly automated, previously difficult analytical activities may become accessible through AI assistance, and new responsibilities may emerge around problem formulation, contextualization, evaluation, coordination, communication, and effective use of AI capabilities. Workforce development can therefore increasingly focus on how people learn to reorganize their own capabilities around a changing set of available agentic capabilities.

## Job Seekers Should Develop Skills for a Changing Human–Agent Division of Work

The same principle has important implications for **job finding and career development**. Job seekers may increasingly benefit from demonstrating not only what they can accomplish independently, but also what they can accomplish by appropriately combining their own capabilities with Agentic AI. This does not mean that conventional professional skills become irrelevant. Domain knowledge, communication, analytical judgment, creativity, collaboration, responsibility, and practical experience remain important because they influence how effectively a person can define problems, interpret circumstances, assess AI-generated results, and make consequential decisions. However, these capabilities can increasingly be complemented by the ability to identify suitable opportunities for AI assistance, formulate tasks clearly, select and use agentic capabilities, provide appropriate context, evaluate intermediate and final outputs, and intervene when automated execution becomes unreliable or inappropriate. Employability can therefore depend increasingly on a person's ability to operate effectively within a **changing human–agent capability system**, rather than on either purely human competence or AI usage considered separately.

## Career Development Should Focus on Adaptability Rather Than a Permanent Skill Boundary

Because AI capabilities are developing rapidly, attempting to identify a permanent list of “human-only skills” and “AI-only skills” may provide limited long-term value. Activities that currently require substantial human expertise may become increasingly supported by agentic systems, while the introduction of those systems may simultaneously create new human responsibilities that were previously unnecessary. A more durable career strategy is therefore to develop the ability to **adapt the boundary itself**. Individuals can continually assess which activities they should perform directly, which can be augmented by AI, which can be delegated substantially to agents, and which require combined human–agent execution. This assessment should change as models improve, new tools become available, organizational requirements evolve, and individuals develop new knowledge and experience. The valuable human capability is consequently not only possession of a particular fixed skill set, but also the ability to reorganize one's skills and responsibilities around an evolving technological environment.

## Toward an Evolving Human–Agent Work System

The long-term integration of human skills and agentic skills is therefore better understood as the development of an **evolving human–agent work system** rather than a simple replacement of human labor with AI. Humans contribute objectives, contextual understanding, domain experience, interpersonal capabilities, judgment, responsibility, and the ability to respond to circumstances that extend beyond the information represented within an AI system. Agentic systems contribute scalable model capabilities that can comprehend information, reason over problems, generate and evaluate alternatives, write code, use tools, coordinate operations, and repeatedly perform well-defined cognitive activities. The boundary between these contributions can move continuously as capabilities and circumstances change. Effective workplaces and successful job seekers can therefore benefit from neither rejecting Agentic AI nor depending on it unconditionally, but from continually determining how **human skills and agentic skills can be recombined, reallocated, and refined according to the evolving requirements of real work**.
