# 1. Working with Agentic AI: Logistics Analyst

*How human skills and agentic skills are dynamically integrated and coordinated across logistics analysis responsibilities*

## 1.1. Logistics Analyst as an Occupation

A **logistics analyst** analyzes and optimizes supply chain and logistics operations to reduce costs, improve efficiency, and ensure reliable delivery performance. This occupation supports the movement of goods across suppliers, manufacturing nodes, warehousing facilities, and end customers by analyzing transportation routes, inventory levels, distribution networks, and operational workflows that support scalability, resilience, and cost-effectiveness. To ensure logistics strategies support long-term organizational objectives, the analyst must understand how demand variability, carrier capacity, warehouse constraints, fuel costs, geopolitical factors, and service-level agreements collectively shape the supply chain landscape.

Typical responsibilities of a logistics analyst include:

* supply chain network and route optimization;
* inventory and warehouse operations analysis;
* transportation and freight management;
* demand forecasting and capacity planning;
* supplier and vendor performance evaluation;
* logistics cost analysis and financial reporting;
* risk management and supply chain resilience; and
* process improvement and logistics system integration.

These responsibilities are fulfilled through distinct tasks and subtasks, each requiring one or more actions with specific skill requirements. Human skills and agentic skills both contribute to these responsibilities, but their levels and forms of involvement differ according to the responsibility, task, action, network scope, operational context, system complexity, technical environment, and expected outcome.

## 1.2. Human Skills and Agentic Skills in Logistics Analysis

Logistics analysis increasingly integrates both **human skills** and **agentic skills**.

Human skills are reusable capabilities possessed and developed by logistics analysts. They include systems thinking, operational reasoning, spatial analysis, supplier negotiation, risk assessment, process design, cost-benefit evaluation, stakeholder communication, facilitation, contextual interpretation, and professional judgment.

Agentic skills are reusable capabilities available to AI agents through their underlying models, contextual information, instructions, constraints, tools, and computational resources. In logistics analysis, these skills include information retrieval, route calculation, inventory data extraction, freight rate comparison, demand pattern recognition, anomaly detection, schedule generation, cost aggregation, scenario simulation, and tool use.

Both skill types contribute to the same occupational responsibilities, but their involvement varies by action. Optimizing a distribution network relies primarily on human understanding of service-level agreements and operational constraints; an AI agent then uses agentic skills to calculate routing alternatives and extract historical transit times. In inventory analysis, the analyst first establishes the service-level targets, and an AI agent subsequently classifies stock-keeping units (SKUs), maps warehouse capacities, and identifies stockout risks. For freight auditing, an AI agent compares invoiced rates against contracted rates, while the analyst interprets systemic discrepancies, evaluates carrier relationships, and determines the appropriate operational response. Across these actions, human and agentic skills are activated sequentially or iteratively based on task requirements, with the human professional maintaining oversight and final judgment.

The relationship between these skills is not a fixed division where certain responsibilities permanently belong to humans and others to AI. Instead, human and agentic involvement shifts across tasks and between different actions within the same task.

## 1.3. Dynamic Human–Agentic Skill Integration and Collaboration in Logistics Analysis

The work of a logistics analyst can be understood as a structured set of responsibilities fulfilled through tasks and subtasks, which are executed through actions requiring appropriate configurations of human and agentic skills.

An **occupation** provides the overall professional context. For a logistics analyst, this context encompasses optimizing supply chain networks, managing transportation and inventory, evaluating vendor performance, reducing operational costs, and maintaining resilience across distribution channels.

A **responsibility** represents an expected area of professional work or an outcome that the logistics analyst accomplishes or maintains. Responsibilities such as route optimization, inventory analysis, freight management, and process improvement each contain multiple tasks.

A **task** is a **context-dependent unit of work** used to fulfill part or all of a responsibility. When a task is too broad or complex for reliable execution, it is decomposed into smaller **subtasks**.

Tasks and subtasks are executed through one or more **actions**. Actions represent **reusable and configurable operations** such as calculating route efficiency, mapping warehouse zones, comparing freight bids, identifying inventory bottlenecks, auditing shipping invoices, or simulating supply chain disruptions.

Each action has **skill requirements**. These requirements describe the capabilities needed for effective and reliable execution. Depending on the action and its context, human skills, agentic skills, or an integration of both can satisfy these requirements.

The skills available for logistics analysis reside in a **skill space** containing both human skills and agentic skills. Relevant skills in this space can serve as candidate skills for executing the actions required by a particular task. A human skill in operational reasoning, for example, can serve as a candidate for evaluating the organizational impact of a warehouse consolidation, while an agentic skill in cost aggregation can serve as a candidate for calculating the financial implications of that consolidation.

Candidate skills are evaluated relative to the requirements of the action and its occupational and task context. Appropriate skills are then selected and organized into a **skill configuration** for execution. Depending on these requirements, a configuration may rely mainly on human skills, mainly on agentic skills, or on an integrated arrangement of both.

### 1.3.1. Skills Competition and Skills Collaboration

Two important relationships can occur between human skills and agentic skills when constructing a skill configuration: **skills competition** and **skills collaboration**.

**Skills competition** occurs when both a human skill and an agentic skill can satisfy the same or similar requirements for an action, but simultaneous application of both is unnecessary. Under a particular occupational and task context, one skill type can provide a better fit for the action than the other.

For example, both a logistics analyst and an AI agent can compare standard freight bids for a routine shipping lane. When the lane characteristics are explicit, the historical data is sufficiently complete, and conformance can be evaluated through defined cost criteria, the agentic skill provides fast and consistent execution. When the selection involves strategic carrier partnerships, volatile fuel surcharges, or a justified exception to standard routing guides, the analyst's human skills provide the contextual reasoning required for the action.

The purpose of skills competition is not to establish whether human or agentic skills are generally superior. Rather, it identifies which available skill provides the better fit for a particular action under particular conditions.

**Skills collaboration** occurs when one skill type alone cannot effectively satisfy the requirements of a task or its actions, or when combining complementary human and agentic skills produces stronger task performance.

Collaboration does not require human and agentic skills to operate simultaneously. Instead, they are activated at different stages of action execution in a **sequential, iterative, or recursive manner** according to evolving task requirements.

For example, a logistics analyst first identifies that several regional distribution centers experience overlapping seasonal bottlenecks and determines which operational variables require investigation. An AI agent then examines historical shipment volumes, carrier capacity data, and warehouse throughput records, identifying areas of congestion and underutilized assets. The analyst subsequently interprets these findings in relation to customer delivery expectations and develops several network realignment alternatives. Based on the analyst's direction, the agent next compares the alternatives across defined criteria such as transit time, freight cost, carbon footprint, and facility capacity. Finally, the analyst evaluates the resulting evidence and determines which distribution strategy should guide subsequent operational planning. Throughout this sequence, human and agentic skills are activated iteratively as task requirements evolve, while the analyst directs the analysis and evaluates its outcomes.

This dynamic relationship allows logistics analysts to leverage the complementary strengths of both skill types rather than relying on a permanent allocation of work.

### 1.3.2. Skill Configuration and Task Execution

A **skill configuration** specifies which human skills and agentic skills are used to execute the actions associated with a task or subtask and how those skills are coordinated.

The configuration depends on factors such as action requirements, network complexity, seasonal volatility, fuel cost fluctuations, available carrier capacity, expected reliability, service-level constraints, operational impact, decision consequences, stakeholder expectations, available tools, and intermediate results.

For one action, an agentic skill may independently satisfy the requirements. For another, a human skill may provide the better fit. A complex logistics task may require several configurations across multiple actions, with human and agentic skills repeatedly interacting as the task progresses.

The configuration is therefore dynamic rather than permanent. It may change when the action changes, new supply chain data becomes available, market conditions shift, an agentic result fails to satisfy expected requirements, or human interpretation identifies a new operational direction.

Throughout this process, the logistics analyst remains the professional responsible for directing and controlling the work associated with the occupation and its responsibilities. Agentic AI provides powerful complementary capabilities that expand what the professional can calculate, map, compare, analyze, simulate, audit, and communicate.

### 1.3.3. Task Performance Evaluation and Skill Space Adaptation

The performance produced through a selected skill configuration is evaluated against **predefined evaluation metrics**. These metrics determine whether the human skills, agentic skills, or their integrated configuration are sufficiently capable of performing the relevant task and its required actions.

Evaluation criteria depend on the work being performed. In logistics analysis, they include cost accuracy, delivery reliability, inventory turnover, route efficiency, forecast accuracy, carrier compliance, operational scalability, and other task-specific expectations.

When a selected skill or skill configuration produces performance above the defined threshold, it passes the performance evaluation and is retained for future applications under appropriate conditions. Repeated successful use provides additional evidence about where that skill or configuration performs effectively.

When performance falls below the required threshold, the corresponding skill or configuration is not reused without modification. The relevant human or agentic skills may be refined or reconfigured, existing skills can be combined differently, or new human and agentic skills may be developed or constructed from scratch when the existing skill space lacks adequate capability.

For example, an AI agent first generates baseline demand forecasts for regional warehouses, but task performance evaluation shows that it repeatedly misses volume spikes caused by localized promotional events rather than general market trends. The agentic skill is then refined through improved contextual instructions, integration with marketing calendars, stronger pattern-recognition procedures, or validation against historical promotional lift data. The revised skill is evaluated again, and once it satisfies the required performance criteria, it is retained for appropriate future forecasting tasks. Human skills follow a corresponding development process: when an analyst lacks sufficient knowledge of a newly implemented automated warehouse sorting system, the relevant capability is developed through learning, collaboration, and practical experience before its performance is evaluated through subsequent work.

Refined and newly constructed skills that satisfy the required performance criteria are added to the skill space. In this way, task performance evaluation provides feedback not only for completing current work but also for continuously optimizing and expanding the available skill space.

The skill space is not constructed as an isolated collection of occupation-specific representations. Many human and agentic skills used in logistics analysis are reusable across occupations. Systems thinking, spatial analysis, information retrieval, cost calculation, scenario simulation, anomaly detection, schedule generation, and tool use contribute to work in manufacturing, retail operations, procurement, urban planning, management consulting, and many other fields.

Reusable skills can therefore be represented once and configured differently according to the responsibilities, tasks, actions, and contexts of different occupations. This allows the skill space to serve a growing range of occupations without unnecessarily duplicating or exploding the number of skill representations. Occupational specialization is introduced through task context, action requirements, operational parameters, information sources, tools, constraints, and skill configurations rather than by creating a completely separate skill representation whenever the same underlying capability appears in another occupation.

The resulting process is adaptive:

**occupational responsibility → task and subtask → actions → skill requirements → candidate human and agentic skills → skill configuration → task execution → task performance evaluation → skill refinement, reconfiguration, or construction → optimized and expanded skill space**

This cycle allows human and agentic capabilities to evolve alongside the requirements of logistics analysis work.

## 1.4. Essential Responsibilities of Logistics Analyst

### 1.4.1. Supply Chain Network and Route Optimization

Supply chain network and route optimization establishes the most efficient physical pathways and node configurations required to move goods from origin to destination. This responsibility includes understanding service-level requirements, assessing current network topology, defining optimal hub-and-spoke models, identifying routing inefficiencies, and determining how distribution channels should evolve over time.

Agentic skills contribute to distance calculation, node mapping, transit time extraction, scenario generation, and comparison of routing alternatives. Human skills remain essential for interpreting customer service expectations, balancing competing cost and speed priorities, determining acceptable operational trade-offs, and defining a network strategy that remains realistic within geographic and infrastructural constraints.

For example, a logistics analyst first defines the service-level requirements that the future distribution network must support and identifies the major geographic regions requiring change. An AI agent then examines available node data, historical transit times, and documented shipping volumes, summarizing significant constraints and routing bottlenecks. The analyst interprets these findings and develops several candidate hub-and-spoke network directions. Based on the analyst's criteria, the agent next compares these alternatives across strategic alignment, transit speed, freight cost, and carbon footprint. Finally, the analyst resolves the major trade-offs and establishes the network direction that guides subsequent logistics planning.

| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| strategic network design, geographic reasoning, operational trade-off evaluation, service-level interpretation | distance calculation, node mapping, transit time extraction, scenario generation, comparative analysis | The analyst defines strategic network direction and evaluates major trade-offs, while the AI agent organizes current-state evidence, identifies bottlenecks, and compares routing alternatives. |

### 1.4.2. Inventory and Warehouse Operations Analysis

Inventory and warehouse operations analysis translates organizational sales and procurement strategies into structured representations of stock levels, warehouse space utilization, and material flow. This responsibility includes defining inventory policies, identifying stockout risks, relating SKU velocity to warehouse slotting, and establishing operational views that connect inventory needs with physical storage constraints.

Agentic skills are well suited to extracting transaction data, classifying SKU velocity, mapping warehouse capacities, comparing historical stock levels, and linking inventory records with physical bin locations. Human skills contribute to determining the strategic importance of specific product lines, resolving ambiguous demand signals, defining future-state inventory buffers, and deciding how warehouse layout changes should influence operational efficiency.

For example, an AI agent first extracts relevant transaction data and warehouse throughput records, preparing a preliminary ABC inventory classification and slotting structure. The logistics analyst reviews the structure and identifies areas where upcoming strategic product launches distort the historical velocity patterns. The analyst then restructures the model around the anticipated operational requirements of the new product lines. Based on the revised structure, the agent recalculates safety stock levels and maps the optimal physical bin assignments. Finally, the analyst validates the resulting inventory model and determines which warehouse process changes require operational attention.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| inventory strategy, space utilization reasoning, operational prioritization, demand interpretation, process assessment | transaction extraction, SKU classification, capacity mapping, cross-referencing, slotting documentation | The AI agent accelerates extraction and organization of inventory data, while the analyst defines the operational strategy and determines its physical warehouse significance. |

### 1.4.3. Transportation and Freight Management

Transportation and freight management maintains a coherent view of the carriers, shipping modes, freight rates, and transit lanes used across the supply chain. This responsibility includes tracking carrier performance, identifying consolidation opportunities, examining freight spend, evaluating mode fit, and establishing routing guide priorities.

Agentic skills efficiently classify large freight portfolios, extract rate information, identify load consolidation opportunities, map lane dependencies, summarize transit costs, and identify candidates for modal shifts or carrier renegotiation. Human skills remain essential for understanding strategic carrier relationships, evaluating service-level consequences, determining whether apparent cost savings represent genuine value, and establishing transportation priorities.

For example, the logistics analyst first defines the criteria for reviewing a transportation portfolio, including sustainability targets, transit reliability, cost efficiency, and carrier capacity. An AI agent then classifies the shipping lanes against these criteria and identifies potential areas for load consolidation and modal shifts. The analyst reviews the identified candidates and distinguishes genuine optimization opportunities from cases where cheaper transit modes would unacceptably delay critical manufacturing components. The agent next analyzes the scheduling implications for the selected candidates. Finally, the analyst establishes recommendations for carrier renegotiation, mode shifting, or route consolidation.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| carrier relationship management, mode selection, service-level governance, freight strategy, negotiation judgment | portfolio classification, rate extraction, consolidation detection, lane mapping, cost and transit analysis | The AI agent systematically examines large freight portfolios and identifies potential optimization opportunities, while the analyst determines which transportation changes are appropriate within the wider operational context. |

### 1.4.4. Demand Forecasting and Capacity Planning

Demand forecasting and capacity planning ensures that logistics networks possess the physical and labor capacity required to meet anticipated customer demand without excessive overhead. This responsibility includes analyzing historical sales patterns, adjusting for market variables, identifying capacity bottlenecks, evaluating labor requirements, and establishing operational baselines.

Agentic skills support time-series analysis, pattern recognition, anomaly detection, capacity simulation, and schedule generation. Human skills remain essential for interpreting unquantified market shifts, evaluating the impact of promotional events, resolving conflicting demand signals, and controlling consequential procurement and labor decisions.

For example, an AI agent first analyzes historical sales data and generates baseline statistical forecasts for regional distribution centers. The logistics analyst reviews the forecasts and identifies that upcoming localized marketing campaigns will create demand spikes not captured in the historical baseline. Based on the analyst's adjusted volume expectations, the agent recalculates the required warehouse labor shifts and transportation capacity. Finally, the analyst evaluates the complete capacity plan and determines whether the operational resources should be scaled up, shifted, or augmented with temporary labor.

| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| market intuition, promotional impact assessment, capacity reasoning, operational judgment, labor planning | time-series analysis, pattern recognition, anomaly detection, capacity simulation, schedule generation | The AI agent performs systematic statistical forecasting and capacity calculation, while the analyst interprets market variables and controls the final operational resource allocation. |

### 1.4.5. Supplier and Vendor Performance Evaluation

Supplier and vendor performance evaluation ensures that third-party logistics providers, carriers, and material suppliers align with organizational service standards, compliance requirements, and strategic objectives. This responsibility includes defining key performance indicators (KPIs), tracking service-level agreements (SLAs), identifying compliance deviations, evaluating root causes, and guiding vendors toward appropriate operational corrections.

Agentic skills support SLA tracking, compliance scoring, delivery data aggregation, variance identification, and vendor scorecard generation. Human skills remain essential for investigating the root causes of systemic failures, evaluating justified exceptions, resolving competing vendor constraints, and determining the appropriate strategic response.

For example, the logistics analyst first establishes the KPIs and SLA thresholds relevant to a major transportation vendor. An AI agent then aggregates delivery and tracking data, compares actual performance against the established thresholds, and identifies patterns of late deliveries or damaged freight. The analyst reviews these findings and determines which issues represent systemic operational failures requiring vendor intervention. Based on the analyst's direction, the agent updates the vendor risk scorecards and generates the performance review documentation. Finally, the analyst evaluates the vendor's corrective action plan and determines whether the contract should be renewed, penalized, or terminated.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| relationship evaluation, root-cause investigation, risk mitigation, compliance judgment, strategic negotiation | SLA tracking, data aggregation, variance identification, scorecard generation, compliance checking | The AI agent performs systematic performance tracking and organizes compliance evidence, while the analyst investigates root causes and determines the appropriate strategic vendor response. |

### 1.4.6. Logistics Cost Analysis and Financial Reporting

Logistics cost analysis and financial reporting establishes how transportation, warehousing, and inventory carrying costs should be tracked, allocated, and optimized across the enterprise. This responsibility includes defining cost-to-serve models, auditing freight invoices, identifying billing discrepancies, aligning operational spend with budgets, and ensuring that logistics decisions support financial objectives.

Agentic skills efficiently audit invoices against contracts, identify billing variances, allocate costs to specific product lines, generate financial dashboards, and analyze consistency across documented rate agreements. Human skills remain essential for interpreting the operational drivers of cost variances, determining appropriate budget reallocations, resolving cross-departmental financial conflicts, and ensuring that cost-reduction decisions do not degrade service quality.

For example, an AI agent first audits a month of freight invoices against negotiated carrier contracts and identifies duplicated charges, incorrect accessorial fees, and systemic billing variances. The logistics analyst reviews the resulting financial landscape and determines which discrepancies require immediate carrier disputes and which indicate internal routing guide violations. The analyst then defines the corrective operational parameters. Based on this direction, the agent reallocates the corrected costs to specific product lines and generates the financial variance reports. Finally, the analyst validates the financial impact and presents the cost-to-serve analysis to organizational leadership.

### 1.4.7. Risk Management and Supply Chain Resilience

Risk management and supply chain resilience ensures that logistics networks incorporate appropriate contingency plans, disruption monitoring, and operational buffers to withstand unexpected external shocks. This responsibility includes evaluating network vulnerabilities, defining risk tolerances, examining the implications of port congestion or material shortages, identifying single points of failure, and ensuring that logistics strategies remain compatible with enterprise continuity expectations.

Agentic skills support disruption monitoring, scenario simulation, bottleneck identification, alternative routing generation, and consistency checking across contingency plans. Human skills remain essential for interpreting geopolitical and environmental risks, defining acceptable operational exposure, resolving conflicts between resilience costs and efficiency goals, and determining the appropriate contingency response.

For example, the logistics analyst first establishes the risk tolerances and identifies critical single points of failure within the global supply chain. An AI agent then monitors global weather patterns, port congestion data, and geopolitical alerts, simulating the operational impact of potential disruptions on critical shipping lanes. The analyst reviews the simulated scenarios and determines which vulnerabilities require immediate contingency routing rather than passive monitoring. Based on the analyst's decisions, the agent generates alternative routing guides and calculates the cost impact of these diversions. Finally, the analyst assesses the residual risk and finalizes the supply chain resilience playbook.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| contingency planning, geopolitical reasoning, risk tolerance definition, resilience strategy, operational judgment | disruption monitoring, scenario simulation, bottleneck identification, routing generation, impact calculation | The AI agent performs systematic disruption monitoring and simulates risk scenarios, while the analyst interprets external threats and determines the appropriate operational contingency response. |

### 1.4.8. Process Improvement and Logistics System Integration

Process improvement and logistics system integration connects operational workflows with enterprise technology systems, ensuring that data flows seamlessly between order management, warehouse execution, and transportation platforms. This responsibility includes mapping current workflows, identifying redundant approvals, defining system integration requirements, maintaining operational roadmaps, and adjusting processes as business and technology conditions change.

Agentic skills support process mining, system mapping, workflow simulation, bottleneck identification, and preparation of technical configuration requirements. Human skills remain essential for redesigning workflows to eliminate operational friction, interpreting stakeholder concerns, explaining process trade-offs, and maintaining alignment between strategic logistics direction and system capabilities.

For example, operational leaders first request that the order-to-delivery process be accelerated. The logistics analyst defines which workflow steps and system handoffs must be reconsidered. An AI agent then maps the current digital process, identifies redundant approval bottlenecks, and simulates alternative automated workflows. The analyst reviews the alternatives, discusses the operational trade-offs with warehouse and transportation stakeholders, and determines which process changes are feasible without undermining inventory accuracy. Based on the agreed direction, the agent generates the system configuration requirements and updates the process documentation. Finally, the analyst validates the integrated process and ensures that the operational workflow remains aligned with organizational expectations.

## 1.6. Working as a Logistics Analyst with Agentic AI

Agentic AI is becoming an increasingly powerful component of logistics analysis. Logistics analysts should therefore adopt and integrate AI agents as complementary participants in professional workflows rather than treating them only as occasional tools for freight auditing, route calculation, or data extraction.

This shift does not reduce the importance of logistics expertise. Human professionals remain responsible for the occupation and its corresponding responsibilities. They understand operational strategy, define network topologies, establish inventory policies, evaluate cross-functional consequences, determine acceptable service-cost trade-offs, interpret risk and compliance requirements, communicate with stakeholders, control consequential supply chain decisions, evaluate task performance, and decide how human and agentic skills are configured and improved.

At the same time, agentic skills greatly expand the volume and variety of logistics information that can be processed. AI agents retrieve and organize extensive transit data, analyze large freight and inventory portfolios, map warehouse capacities, compare routing alternatives, identify billing inconsistencies, evaluate SLA alignment, prepare operational models and documentation, and synthesize evidence across multiple supply chain nodes at a scale difficult to achieve through direct human effort alone.

Strong logistics analysis practice is therefore not based on maximizing human work or agentic work independently. It is based on finding appropriate configurations of human skills and agentic skills for the responsibilities, tasks, subtasks, and actions being performed.

Where a human skill and an agentic skill can independently perform the same action, skills competition identifies which is better suited to the current context. Where work requires complementary capabilities, skills collaboration results in human and agentic skills being activated sequentially, iteratively, or recursively to achieve stronger task performance than either skill type achieves alone.

Task performance evaluation then determines whether selected skills and configurations satisfy predefined expectations. Successful skills are retained and reused, while inadequate skills are refined, reconfigured, or newly developed. The resulting capabilities return to an increasingly useful skill space that supports logistics analysis and, where capabilities are reusable, other occupations as well.

For a logistics analyst, working effectively with agentic AI means treating AI as a powerful complementary capability integrated into everyday professional routines while maintaining human control of occupational responsibilities. Through appropriate skill selection, competition, collaboration, evaluation, and continuous improvement, human professionals and agentic AI work together to achieve logistics operations that are more coherent, scalable, adaptable, efficient, resilient, and strategically aligned.
