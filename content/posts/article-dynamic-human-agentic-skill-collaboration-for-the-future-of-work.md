## 1.1. Framework structure and essential components

The Dynamic Human-Agentic Skill Collaboration Framework (DyCo Skills) provides a systematic way to understand how human and agentic AI skills can be applied in professional work. The framework is organized into two interconnected layers: the work decomposition layer and the human-agentic skill allocation layer.

The work decomposition layer describes how work is organized. An occupation includes multiple responsibilities; each responsibility is fulfilled through one or more tasks; and tasks can be further divided into subtasks when a finer level of analysis is necessary and meaningful. This hierarchy identifies the components of work to which human and agentic skills can be allocated.

The human–agentic skill allocation layer describes how human and agentic skills are represented, compared, selected, combined, and adjusted to perform these components of work. For each relevant task or subtask, the capability requirements are first identified. Available human skills and agentic skills are represented within a common skills space, which provides a vector-based representation for comparing their functional similarities while preserving differences that may matter under particular working conditions. Capability requirements are then mapped to suitable skills, and a skill configuration is selected for execution.

After execution, the result is evaluated. When performance gaps, changing conditions, or other relevant issues are identified, reallocation may occur by reconsidering the mapping or skill configuration. Execution, evaluation, and reallocation can therefore form a repeated cycle as the work progresses.

The two layers serve different but complementary functions. The work decomposition layer determines what the work consists of and at what level it should be analyzed, while the human-agentic skill allocation layer determines how human and agentic skills are matched, combined, and adjusted to perform that work. Together, they provide the basic architecture for human-agentic skill integration. The following sections explain each part of this architecture in greater detail.

## 1.2. Work decomposition as the foundation for dynamic integration

The work decomposition layer is more than a way to describe a job. In DHASIF, it provides the foundation for deciding how human and agentic skills should be allocated.

Traditional job analysis often describes work in terms of relatively stable occupations, responsibilities, and tasks. DHASIF retains this familiar structure but uses it in a more dynamic way. Breaking a task into subtasks can reveal different capability requirements within the same task. This makes it possible to use different combinations of human and agentic skills for different parts of the work rather than treating the entire task as a single unit.

For example, one task might involve collecting information, interpreting unusual cases, and preparing a final report. These activities may require different capabilities. Information collection may be well suited to agentic skills, while unusual cases may require greater human judgment. Report preparation may involve a combination of both. Decomposition makes these differences visible and allows skills to be allocated accordingly.

Work structures can also change over time. Responsibilities may evolve as technologies, regulations, customer expectations, and professional standards change. Tasks may appear, disappear, merge, or divide into new tasks. Their frequency, complexity, and capability requirements may also change. Because DHASIF is based on a decomposable structure rather than a fixed list of tasks, the framework can accommodate these changes without requiring its basic logic to be redesigned.

The appropriate level of decomposition is not fixed. Some tasks are simple or sufficiently uniform to be treated as single units. Others need to be divided into several subtasks because different parts require meaningfully different capabilities.

A practical rule is to decompose a task further when doing so reveals important differences in capability requirements or creates useful opportunities for different skill configurations. Over-decomposition can create unnecessary coordination and complexity, while under-decomposition can hide useful opportunities for human–agentic collaboration.

DHASIF therefore does not replace conventional occupational structures. Instead, it uses them as a flexible foundation for matching work requirements with available human and agentic skills. The occupation–responsibility–task–subtask hierarchy remains familiar, but it becomes a dynamic structure that can support different skill configurations as work conditions, requirements, and technologies change.

## 1.3. Skills space, capability mapping, and configuration selection

Once a task or subtask has been identified, the next question is how it should be performed. Before selecting particular human or agentic skills, the capability requirements of the work must first be identified.

Capability requirements describe what the work requires. For example, a unit of work may require rapid information processing, physical coordination, creative thinking, careful judgment, communication ability, or knowledge of a particular law. Identifying these requirements before selecting skills helps avoid assuming in advance that either a human or an AI agent should perform the work.

The abilities available to meet these requirements are called skills. In DHASIF, these skills come from two main sources: human skills and agentic skills.

Human skills are abilities provided by people. They include abilities such as reasoning, communication, empathy, physical coordination, professional judgment, experience, and creative problem-solving.

Agentic skills are abilities provided by AI agents that can perform or support goal-directed activities. Depending on the system, these skills may include searching documents, organizing large amounts of data, monitoring information for changes, generating structured text, analyzing information, or carrying out sequences of digital actions.

The labels human and agentic identify the source of a skill. They do not by themselves indicate that one type of skill is better than the other. The suitability of a skill depends on how well it meets the capability requirements of the work under the relevant conditions.

Human and agentic skills are represented within a common skills space. The skills space is a vector representation space in which each skill is represented through multiple dimensions. Some dimensions describe the essential functions a skill can perform, while others describe characteristics that affect how the skill performs or whether it is appropriate in a particular context.

Representing human and agentic skills in the same skills space makes comparison possible. When a human skill and an agentic skill perform the same or similar functions, their representations may be close with respect to relevant functional dimensions. For example, both a human researcher and an AI agent may have skills for searching documents, identifying relevant information, and summarizing findings. Their skills can therefore be compared in terms of these shared functions.

At the same time, the skills space preserves meaningful distinctions between human and agentic skills. Two skills that are close in their functional dimensions may still differ substantially in contextual understanding, processing speed, consistency, explainability, cost, privacy implications, accountability conditions, adaptability to unusual situations, or other characteristics. Functional similarity therefore does not mean that two skills are identical or interchangeable.

The importance of these differences depends on the context. Processing speed may be especially important for a high-volume routine task, while contextual understanding and accountability may carry greater weight in a high-stakes professional decision. The same pair of human and agentic skills may therefore be highly similar for one subtask but meaningfully different for another.

The skills space consequently supports context-aware comparison. Rather than asking only whether two skills are generally similar, the framework considers how well each skill satisfies the capability requirements of a particular unit of work under its current conditions and constraints.

After the capability requirements have been identified, a systematic mapping process connects them to candidate human and agentic skills in the skills space. This mapping provides the analytical bridge between what the work requires and the skills available to satisfy those requirements.

The mapping considers three main criteria. Functional fit asks whether a particular human or agentic skill can reliably satisfy the relevant capability requirement under the current working conditions. Constraint compatibility asks whether using the skill is consistent with relevant organizational policies, legal requirements, safety rules, privacy requirements, and accountability arrangements. Interaction coherence asks whether multiple human and agentic skills can work together effectively without unnecessary conflict, duplication, or poorly managed handoffs.

Sometimes no single skill can fully satisfy a capability requirement. In such cases, several skills may be combined, the work may be further decomposed, or the proposed approach may need to be reconsidered. The mapping process therefore identifies viable ways in which available skills can meet the capability requirements rather than simply assigning each requirement to the closest skill in the skills space.

Based on the mapping and comparison, a skill configuration is selected. A skill configuration specifies the human and agentic skills used for execution and how their contributions are arranged.

A configuration may be human-dominant, combined, or agentic-dominant, depending on the capability requirements, context, and constraints. For example, a combined configuration might use agentic skills to gather and organize information while human skills provide contextual judgment and final interpretation. These categories describe the balance of contributions rather than fixed rules about which type of skill should perform particular kinds of work.

The skill configuration is therefore the result of capability mapping and contextual comparison rather than an assumption made before the requirements and available skills have been examined.

## 1.4. Dynamic collaboration in execution, evaluation, and reallocation

Skill allocation is not necessarily a one-time decision. In DHASIF, allocation is dynamic, meaning that the roles and contributions of human and agentic skills can change as work progresses.

Different subtasks within the same task may use different skill configurations. A configuration may also need to change during execution if new information becomes available, an unexpected problem occurs, or working conditions change. Dynamic allocation allows the configuration to respond to these changes rather than requiring the original arrangement to remain fixed.

Execution is therefore followed by evaluation. Evaluation determines whether the result meets the relevant capability requirements and performance criteria. Depending on the work, this may include checking accuracy, completeness, safety, quality, compliance, or suitability for the intended purpose.

Evaluation can be performed in different ways. A human may review the work, an AI agent may conduct automated checks, or established workplace procedures may be used. Human and agentic skills may also contribute together. For example, an AI agent may identify possible inconsistencies while a human determines whether those inconsistencies are important in the specific context.

If the evaluation shows that the result is satisfactory, the workflow can continue. If the result is incomplete, incorrect, unsuitable, or affected by changed conditions, the process may enter reallocation.

Reallocation means reconsidering how the work should be performed. It may involve repeating the subtask, improving instructions to an AI agent, supplying additional information, dividing the work into smaller units, adding human review, selecting different skills from the skills space, or changing the balance between human and agentic contributions.

For example, suppose an AI-generated analysis performs well on routine cases but does not adequately address an unusual situation. The routine portions may continue to use an agentic-dominant configuration, while the unusual portion is reallocated to a combined or human-dominant configuration.

Evaluation may also reveal new information about the suitability of the selected skills. Reallocation can therefore involve not only changing the configuration but also reconsidering how capability requirements are mapped to skills under the current context.

The operational cycle of DHASIF can be summarized as follows:

Identify the unit of work → identify capability requirements → map requirements to human and agentic skills in the skills space → select a skill configuration → execute the work → evaluate the result → continue or reallocate as needed.

This cycle allows skill configurations to adapt as requirements, conditions, available skills, and performance information change.

## 1.5. Constraints, authority, accountability, and professional capability

Skill configuration depends not only on what human and agentic skills are technically capable of doing but also on the conditions surrounding the work.

Relevant factors may include the complexity of the work, the clarity of available information and instructions, time requirements, and the consequences of error. The expertise of the human professional also matters. For example, an experienced professional who understands both the subject matter and the capabilities and limitations of AI agents may be able to use agentic skills differently from a less experienced user.

Organizational policies, privacy requirements, safety regulations, professional standards, and laws may also restrict how agentic skills can be used. A technically possible configuration is therefore not automatically an appropriate configuration. These conditions influence how skills are compared and mapped and which skill configurations are suitable for execution.

This leads to an important distinction among capability, authority, and accountability.

Capability refers to the ability to perform an activity. Authority refers to the recognized right or permission to make or approve a decision. Accountability refers to responsibility for a decision or outcome under the relevant organizational, professional, ethical, or legal arrangements.

These concepts should not be treated as identical. An AI agent may be technically capable of performing much of a unit of work without being authorized to make the final decision. In some professional settings, laws, regulations, organizational rules, or professional standards may require an authorized human professional to approve or take responsibility for particular decisions. The exact arrangement depends on the occupation, organization, and applicable rules.

These distinctions also explain why human and agentic skills that are functionally similar may not be interchangeable. Two skills may be close in the skills space with respect to their ability to perform a function but differ substantially in dimensions related to authority, accountability, privacy, explainability, or other contextual requirements. These differences must be considered when capability requirements are mapped to skills and when a configuration is selected.

Access to AI also changes how professional capability can be understood. A professional's ability to perform work may depend not only on personal knowledge and skills but also on access to agentic skills and the ability to use them effectively.

From the perspective of DHASIF, professional capability in an AI-enhanced workplace therefore includes the professional's own human skills, the agentic skills available to support the work, and the ability to coordinate these resources appropriately.

This coordinating capability includes knowing how to decompose work, identify capability requirements, compare and map human and agentic skills, select suitable configurations, evaluate results, and make adjustments when necessary. As AI agents develop, this coordinating role may become increasingly important in many forms of professional work.

## 1.6. The evolving boundary of work and core framework logic

DHASIF assumes that the boundary between human and agentic contributions can change over time. A subtask that depends heavily on human skills today may become more suitable for agentic execution as AI capabilities improve. Conversely, a subtask that is usually handled effectively by agentic skills may require greater human involvement when an unusual situation occurs, results become uncertain, or the consequences of error increase.

External changes can also shift this boundary. New laws, organizational policies, customer expectations, professional standards, technologies, or changes in available human and agentic skills may alter which skill configurations are appropriate.

The skills space provides a way to represent this evolving capability landscape. As human or agentic capabilities change, their representations in the skills space can also change. New skills can be added, existing skills can develop, and the similarities and differences between human and agentic skills can be reassessed. Changes in the context of work can also alter which dimensions of the skills space are most important for a particular subtask.

The framework therefore does not divide work into permanent categories of “human work” and “AI work.” Nor does it assume that functional similarity between a human skill and an agentic skill automatically makes them substitutes. Instead, it provides an adaptable process for comparing and combining available skills according to the capability requirements, context, and constraints of the work.

The core logic of DHASIF can be summarized as follows:

Occupation → responsibilities → tasks → subtasks

For each relevant unit of work:

Capability requirements → skills space → capability–skill mapping → skill configuration → execution → evaluation → reallocation when needed

The first sequence represents the work decomposition layer. It determines what the work consists of and the level at which it should be analyzed. The second sequence represents the human-agentic skill allocation layer. It determines how capability requirements are matched to available human and agentic skills, how those skills are configured for execution, and how the configuration can be adjusted in response to evaluation and changing conditions.

Within the second layer, the skills space provides the common vector representation needed to compare human and agentic skills. Functional dimensions make skills with the same or similar functions comparable, while other dimensions preserve differences that may matter in a particular context. Capability–skill mapping then uses this information to determine which skills, or combinations of skills, are suitable for the unit of work.

In this way, DHASIF extends familiar workplace structures into AI-enhanced professional work without assuming that either humans or AI agents should always take priority. The appropriate configuration depends on the requirements of the work, the human and agentic skills available, the relevant similarities and differences between those skills, the context and constraints under which they are used, and the results observed during execution. The framework therefore provides a systematic basis for human-agentic skill integration while maintaining appropriate attention to professional oversight, authority, and accountability.
